# ULTRA CREATIVE MISREADING № 2
### *In the Mood for Love*, dir. Wong Kar-wai (2000)

## Tonight's Card

Hong Kong, 1962. A man and a woman rent rooms in neighboring apartments and slowly work out a terrible thing: their spouses are sleeping with each other. So they do something stranger than revenge. They rehearse it — playing their own cheating partners, scripting how the affair must have begun, practicing a confrontation they will never have. They pass each other on the stairs to the noodle stall in slow motion, again and again, to the same looping song. They never sleep together. Years later, one of them whispers a secret into a hole in a ruined wall and seals it with mud. Three readings have come tonight to fight over what all of that means. Two are beautiful. Two will lose. The prize never changes: only the reading that can walk through the whole film without once looking away gets to keep the belt.

## The Rules

A *creative misreading* is a bold, theory-armed interpretation that deliberately shoves a film past its polite, intended meaning. We are not asking what Wong Kar-wai meant. We are asking which engine this film will actually run at full power. All three contenders are strong — weak readings don't get a walk-out. But the tournament has one merciless law: **a reading stays alive only as long as it can cross every major scene without flinching.** The moment it has to skip a scene, or wave it off, or quietly soften it into something more comfortable, that's the fold. It's out.

---

## Contender I — "The Unseen Lovers"

**The walk-out.** This reading owns the empty center of the frame. The cheating spouses are *in* this movie — they drive its entire plot — and yet we essentially never see them. A husband is a voice on the phone, the back of a head, a hand. A wife is a handbag, a tie, a coat going out the door. Wong builds the most consequential couple in the film entirely out of objects and off-screen sound. The two people the story is actually about are a hole punched in the middle of every scene.

**Concept Card.** The reading reaches for Jacques Lacan and his idea of the *objet petit a* — the "little object a," which Lacan introduced across his 1960s seminars as the never-attainable thing that *causes* our desire without ever satisfying it. It is not the goal of wanting; it is the missing piece that keeps us wanting at all. Behind it stands an even harsher term, *das Ding* — "the Thing" — Lacan's name for a primal lost object, an emptiness at the core of the psyche that every later love is a doomed attempt to fill. Lay this over the film and it snaps into place: the unseen spouses are the void at the dead center, the lack that organizes everyone's longing around it.

**The case.** It is a beautiful fit, and for a while it looks unbeatable. Desire in this film really does circle an absence. The tragic charge comes from a thing kept just off-screen, just out of reach — and isn't that *exactly* Lacan's claim, that we are wired to want precisely what we cannot have? The unseen lovers become the perfect engine: a hole that pulls every glance, every cheongsam, every slow walk down the corridor into orbit. The film hides its central couple because desire's true object was always meant to stay hidden.

**The fold.** It flinches at the rehearsal where Su breaks down. Watch what happens. Chow and Su are *play-acting* their spouses, scripting the affair, and Su suddenly weeps: "I didn't expect it to hurt so much." The Lacanian reading has an answer ready: there is a real desire here, it has a missing object, and the pain is the gap where the object should be. But that answer needs a real love underneath the performance — a true feeling with one piece tragically absent. The scene shows the opposite. The feeling isn't hiding behind the role-play, waiting to be reached. The feeling is *produced by* the role-play; it has no existence before someone starts pretending. "The Unseen Lovers" domesticates the absence into a tidy structural slot — an empty chair the viewer is invited to fill in. But the film's move is colder than a missing object. The reading still assumes a love with one piece gone. The film is staging a love with no original at all.

**Verdict.** Eliminated, with respect. It found the void at the center and mistook it for a vacancy — a seat someone left, rather than a seat that was never occupied.

## Contender II — "Rehearsing Forward"

**The walk-out.** This reading owns the rehearsals themselves, and the strange hope inside them. Chow and Su don't just mourn. They *practice*. They sit in the noodle shop and act out how the affair must have started. They run the confrontation: you play my husband, I'll be your wife, ask me where I've been. And in their practiced restraint there is a promise — "We won't be like them." They are trying to live through the betrayal by performing it ahead of themselves, as if going through the motions could deliver them somewhere better than where their spouses ended up.

**Concept Card.** The reading turns to Søren Kierkegaard and his idea of *repetition* — *Gjentagelse* — from his 1843 book of that name. Kierkegaard set repetition against *recollection*. Recollection looks backward; it mourns a past you can only ever lose again. Repetition looks forward; it is the daring wager that you can get something back by re-enacting it ahead of you, that life is lived not by remembering but by recovering — moving into the future to reclaim what mattered. Kierkegaard built the idea as a hope: the self that risks repetition can *receive itself back*, restored and whole, on the far side.

**The case.** On paper it is the most hopeful reading in the room, and the most humane. Chow and Su's reenactments stop looking morbid and start looking brave. They are not wallowing in their spouses' betrayal; they are repeating it forward — rehearsing the pain in order to come out the other side as people who did *not* become what was done to them. Every staged confrontation is a wager on a future self, an attempt to walk through the fire ahead of time and arrive, finally, somewhere they can live.

**The fold.** It flinches at the empty room. Kierkegaard's repetition needs a destination — a self that arrives, that gets itself back, restored. This film grants no arrival, ever. Go to the late scenes, 1966: Chow has taken the job in Singapore and gone; the old neighbors have scattered; Su comes back to the apartment building where the two were once neighbors and finds him long gone, the rooms passed to strangers. There is no reunion, no recovery, no self received back whole. She stands in a place full of other people's lives, and the one she came for isn't in it. The whole film is built this way — corridors crossed at offset moments, a near-touch in the rain that doesn't land, a phone that says nothing, encounter after encounter that just misses. "Rehearsing Forward" needs the spiral to close somewhere. The film keeps the spiral open on purpose; it bends every rehearsal toward a meeting that never comes. The reading has to keep looking away from each missed encounter and from that final empty room — the precise places where its promised arrival was supposed to be, and isn't.

**Verdict.** Eliminated, with respect. It bet on a forward-repetition that gets the self back. The film spends two hours proving there is no back to get.

## The Final — "A Love That Was Never There"

Go back to the rehearsal in the noodle shop. Chow and Su, playing their own spouses, scripting an affair that is not theirs — and Su crumbling: "I didn't expect it to hurt so much." Hold that. Two people feeling something real, with total conviction, while quoting a love that belongs to other people.

Now notice how both losers fell the same way. The Lacanian kept a real desire with a missing object. The Kierkegaardian kept a real self heading toward recovery. Both still assumed a true, present love sitting *behind* the performances, waiting to be reached or restored. The champion's entire move is that there is no behind.

**Concept Card I.** Jacques Derrida coined *hauntology* in *Specters of Marx* (1993) — a pun on *ontology*, the study of what *is*. Hauntology is the study of what neither fully is nor isn't: the ghost, the trace, the thing that haunts the present without ever being present. Derrida used it to unseat a long Western assumption that reality is built on solid, present things; his ghost is more basic than any thing, coming *before* presence and conditioning it. Two tools from the same workshop carry the load here. The *trace* (from *Of Grammatology*, 1967) is a mark with no clean original behind it — every mark already points to another, so there is no pure first version anywhere. And *iterability*: a sign means only because it can be repeated and cited, lifted out of any one moment. Derrida's word for the consequence is *différance* — meaning is always both different from the last instance and deferred to the next, so it never settles into a final, present sense. Together they break the dream of an origin. There are only copies citing copies.

**Concept Card II.** Last, the *crypt* and *restance*. The crypt is a term from the psychoanalysts Nicolas Abraham and Maria Torok, which Derrida took up: a sealed inner vault where something is neither spoken nor destroyed but walled off — kept as a secret, intact only so long as it stays unreadable. *Restance* is Derrida's word for what *remains* — a mark's stubborn capacity to survive and stay legible after its origin, its sender, and its whole original context are gone. The mark outlives the moment that made it; what persists is a leftover cut loose from any source.

Now watch this reading walk the whole film, including both scenes that knocked the others out.

**The rehearsal** — where "The Unseen Lovers" flinched — is the origin of the love, and it is already a quotation. Chow and Su's first real intimacy is not a confession but a *reconstruction*: "How do you think it started?" — each of them playing the other's cheating spouse. Their feeling doesn't precede the performance and hide behind it. The performance *makes* the feeling; it arrives already secondhand, a trace of someone else's affair. So when Su weeps — "I didn't expect it to hurt so much" — that is not a real emotion leaking out from under a mask. It is the mask discovering it has no face beneath it. The love is a copy with no original, and the pain is completely real anyway. That is the trace: a mark that moves you, with nothing authentic standing behind it.

**The empty room** — where "Rehearsing Forward" flinched — stops being a failed arrival and becomes the truth the whole film was built toward. Su comes back to where they were neighbors and finds him gone, the rooms full of strangers. The slow-motion passings on the stairs, looped to the same theme until they detach from any particular day; the cheongsam that changes between scenes that seem to be the same afternoon; the clocks that tick without anything progressing — these aren't a romance stuck on pause. They are *iterability* and *différance* made visible: each repetition has no first instance to repeat, each "now" defers to the next and never lands. There is no meeting being delayed, because there was never a whole the two of them were severed from. The missed encounters aren't accidents on the way to a union. They are the shape of a love that had no present tense to lose.

**The secret at Angkor Wat** closes the case. Years on, in the ruins, Chow leans to a hollow in the stone wall, whispers something into it, and packs the hole with mud and grass. The sentimental reading calls this confession — a love finally spoken, laid to rest. But look at the structure. He doesn't tell us; he *seals* it. This is the crypt exactly: something walled off, neither said nor destroyed, kept as a secret that only stays a secret by staying unreadable. And here is the turn — what he buries has no positive content to bury. You cannot confess a love that never had an origin. So the mud doesn't protect a truth. It entombs an absence *as* an absence — and what stays sealed in the stone is pure *restance*: a remainder that outlives its origin, except here the origin was missing from the start, so the leftover is all there ever was. The ruined temple is the perfect vault for it: monumental, empty, outside the cramped 1962 corridors, a place where a trace can outlast an event it never recorded.

This reading skips nothing. The rehearsal, the empty room, the sealed wall — it crosses every one without softening a single frame. That's why it takes the belt.

## The Decision

Accept "A Love That Was Never There" and *In the Mood for Love* stops being the most exquisite story of restraint ever filmed — and becomes something stranger. The usual reading is the romance of the noble forgo: two good people, a real and available passion, sacrificed for decency. The hauntological reading pulls the floor out. There was never a passion to forgo. Their love is a trace of a trace — generated entirely by citing an affair that wasn't theirs, mourning a thing they never had, modeled on a couple they were never allowed to see. The restraint isn't sacrifice; it's the quiet disclosure that there was no origin under the whole affair to begin with. The film stops *depicting* a love that couldn't be, and starts *demonstrating* that love, in its deepest structure, may always be a haunting — desire as citation, the beloved as a ghost, intimacy as the repeating of something that never had a first time. The title's promise — *the most beautiful, blossoming moment of a life* — turns out to name precisely the thing that was never present to be lived. It survives only as restance: sealed in stone, readable only as the unreadable.

## The Asterisk

Now the honest part, because this is a misreading too — gloriously, on purpose. *Specters of Marx* is a dense political book about Marx's afterlife and the ghosts of a promised future; it is not a manual for a Hong Kong love story, and Wong Kar-wai was almost certainly chasing mood, memory, and heartbreak, not deconstruction. To run this aching, sensuous, deeply *felt* film as a clean seminar in the trace and the crypt is a little cruel to it — to insist there is "no love behind the performance" while Tony Leung's face is plainly breaking with one. You can feel the reading straining against the film's own tenderness; that strain is the price of admission, and we pay it on purpose. *In the Mood for Love* might simply be the truest film ever made about wanting someone you won't let yourself have — no ghosts required. "A Love That Was Never There" wins tonight's tournament. It does not win forever. It stays champion only until a reading walks in that can cross even the scenes *it* held at arm's length — the ones where the feeling looks, against all theory, completely real.
