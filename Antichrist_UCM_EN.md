# ULTRA CREATIVE MISREADING № 1
### *Antichrist*, dir. Lars von Trier (2009)

## Tonight's Card

A grieving couple drives to a cabin in the woods called Eden. A fox tears open its own belly, looks up, and says out loud: *"Chaos reigns."* By the end, one of them is dead, the other is limping away eating berries, and the forest is exactly as it was. Three readings have shown up tonight to fight over what that means. Two of them are gorgeous. Two of them will lose. The prize is the same as always: the only reading allowed to win is the one that can walk through the entire film without once looking away.

## The Rules

A *creative misreading* is a bold, theory-armed interpretation that deliberately shoves a film past its polite meaning. The question is never "what did von Trier intend." The question is "what is the most powerful machine I can build that this film will actually run." All three contenders tonight are strong; weak readings don't get a walk-out. But the tournament has one law and it is merciless: **a reading stays alive only as long as it can cross every major scene without flinching.** The instant it has to skip a scene, or wave it off as "just symbolism," or quietly change the subject — that's the fold. It's out.

---

## Contender I — "Nature Is Evil"

**The walk-out.** This one owns the forest. The cabin named Eden that is anything but. The acorns machine-gunning the roof all night, falling on no one, for no reason. The doe that walks past with a dead fawn hanging half-born from its body. The fox and its verdict, *Chaos reigns.* And the line the wife delivers like a thesis: *"Nature is Satan's church."* No reading looks more obviously right in the first five minutes of the woods.

**Concept Card.** Its deepest version reaches for Friedrich Schelling, whose 1809 freedom essay places a *dark ground* (*der Grund*) at the bottom of things — an unruly, irrational basis beneath reason, beneath order, even beneath God. Schelling needed it to answer a brutal question: if the world comes from a good God, where does evil come from? His answer was that a lawless darkness sits at the root of everything, God included. (He borrowed an even stranger term from the mystic Jakob Böhme — the *Ungrund*, the indifference prior even to that ground.) Drag the dark ground into the woods and the reading writes itself: the forest is that basement of being, finally showing its face — the evil that was always seething under the green.

**The case.** It's a genuinely thrilling fit. The film *wants* you to feel the woods as malevolent — the sound design alone is a slow act of hostility. Reading Eden as the world's dark basement, finally showing its face, explains the dread better than anything a psychology textbook could.

**The fold.** It flinches at the husband's drawing pad. For a full hour the film is about a man with a notebook — a therapist building a pyramid of his wife's fears, running exposure exercises, having her lie in the grass and try to merge with the green. If nature is simply *evil*, that entire hour is set-dressing; the reading has to explain away half the movie as throat-clearing before the real horror. And it mishears the one line it leans on hardest. The fox does not say *Nature is evil.* It says *Chaos reigns.* Evil needs a will and a target — it secretly turns the forest into a villain with a grudge, which puts the human right back at the center as the one being hated. But the acorns fall on no one. The stillbirth is for no one. The woods don't hate us; they *don't know we're here.* "Evil" is the reading flinching away from something far colder.

**Verdict.** Eliminated, with respect. It made the forest a monster because it couldn't bear to make it indifferent.

---

## Contender II — "The Wound of the Feminine"

**The walk-out.** This one owns the body, and the body is where the film spends its blood. The wife came to Eden the previous summer to write a thesis on "gynocide" — the centuries-long slaughter of women as witches. She abandoned it. Now she punishes herself: she crushes her husband's genitals, and in the film's most infamous shot, takes a pair of scissors to her own clitoris. A reading is right there waiting: this is a tragedy about the *feminine made monstrous*, a woman who absorbed the very hatred she was studying and turned its blade on her own flesh.

**Concept Card.** Its engine is Julia Kristeva's *abjection* (from *Powers of Horror*, 1980). The abject is whatever we have to expel to feel like a clean, bounded self — blood, the corpse, the wound, the maternal body. It horrifies us precisely because it shows the border of the self is flimsy. Kristeva built the idea to explain disgust and dread, especially around the maternal. *Antichrist* is soaked in exactly this. You can braid in Luce Irigaray's charge that, in a culture built to a male measure, the feminine gets defined only by what it lacks — never on its own terms.

**The case.** Textually it's strong, maybe the strongest on paper. The thesis on gynocide is *in the film*. The self-mutilation is *in the film*. A woman destroying the disavowed part of her own body, after a summer steeped in images of women burned for their bodies — that almost reads itself.

**The fold.** It flinches at the last shot. This reading needs the wound to *mean* something inside a human drama — the feminine reclaiming, or avenging, or completing the part the world cut away. But the film refuses to pay out. The mutilation delivers no catharsis, no liberation, no transgressive triumph; it just subtracts. And then the epilogue erases the very thing the reading is about: the women who climb the hill at the end have *no faces.* If the film were about the feminine subject — wronged, raging, reclaimed — it could not end by deleting the face, the last sign that anyone is home. Contender II keeps a human subject at the center of the story. The film keeps removing the center.

**Verdict.** Eliminated, with respect. It fought to give the wound a meaning, and the film's whole cruelty is that it won't grant one.

## The Final — "Nature Without Us"

Go back to the first thing the husband does once they reach the woods: he opens a notebook and draws a triangle — a pyramid of her fears, the worst one at the apex. Hold that image. A man, certain the forest is a problem he can diagram.

Now notice how both losers fell the same way. One kept the human as nature's *victim*; the other kept the human as the story's *subject*. Both smuggled us back to the middle of the frame. The champion's entire move is to walk us off it.

**Concept Card I.** Martin Heidegger, in "The Question Concerning Technology" (1954), names the modern way of seeing *Gestell* — "enframing." It's not machines; it's a stance that treats everything as *standing-reserve* (*Bestand*): stock, resources, on call, there to be ordered and used. A river becomes water-power; a forest becomes lumber-in-waiting. Heidegger's warning was that this works *too* well — it gets so good at making things usable that it forgets things can be anything else.

**Concept Card II.** Quentin Meillassoux, in *After Finitude* (*Après la finitude*, 2006; English 2008), names the assumption that has ruled philosophy since Kant *correlationism*: the belief that we never reach a thing *in itself*, only the thing *as it is for us* — that thought and world arrive only as a pair, never apart. He named it in order to break it. His weapon is the *arche-fossil*: physical evidence of a world that existed before any mind — radiation older than life — a reality that plainly owes nothing to a witness. The thing that exists with no *us* attached.

Now watch this reading walk the whole film, including both scenes that knocked the others out.

**The pyramid** — where Contender I flinched — is the husband's *Gestell* in its purest form. He is a therapist; he meets his wife's grief and immediately turns it into stock: rank the fears, label the apex, schedule the exposures, send her into the grass to merge with the green. Grief is never met; it's processed into standing-reserve, a resource to be managed on a timetable. The film opens on a man absolutely certain the world is his to frame.

**"Chaos reigns"** — the line Contender I misheard — is the exact moment that certainty breaks. The fox is not evil and not a symbol of the wife's psyche. It's the instant the forest stops being *his to frame* and announces it was never his at all. The fox is Meillassoux's point made flesh: a world that does not appear *for* a subject, that persists *without* one.

**The mutilations** — where Contender II flinched — stop being about meaning and become about subtraction. She drills a hole through his leg and bolts a heavy grindstone through it: she turns the man who reduced the world to trackable stock into trackable stock himself — a part she can always locate. His own *Gestell* recoils onto him. And the genital violence doesn't *symbolize* anything; it strips the body of the self that thought it was in charge. The wound doesn't add a meaning. It removes a subject.

**The prologue and the epilogue** close the case. In the prologue the parents see everything and change nothing — the slow-motion, the Handel, the beauty, all of it powerless to stop the fall; later he finds the autopsy photos showing she'd been putting the child's shoes on the wrong feet for months. They saw everything; seeing stopped nothing. And the ending: faceless women streaming up the hill while the surviving husband eats berries off the very slope that unmade him. The face — the last proof that the world is being witnessed *by someone, for someone* — is gone. The world simply continues, populated and indifferent. The witness has been removed, and nothing about the world so much as notices.

This reading skips nothing. That's why it takes the belt.

## The Decision

Accept "Nature Without Us" and *Antichrist* stops being a film you interpret and becomes one that interprets you. It is not a psychodrama about grief, not a brief for or against misogyny, not shock for its own sake. von Trier builds something colder: a thought-experiment designed to dismantle the most comfortable assumption we own — that the world is here *for* us, available to be framed, managed, survived on our terms. The husband is that assumption, drawn as a man with a notebook. Eden is the lab where the assumption meets a Nature that was never inside it. And the horror isn't that nature is evil or that the feminine is monstrous — it's that the world goes on, generating and killing, with perfect indifference to whether anyone is left to watch.

## The Asterisk

And now the honest part, because this is a misreading too — gloriously, on purpose. Meillassoux's book is a tight argument about ancestral time and the necessity of contingency; a talking fox is not an arche-fossil, it's a horror-movie effect with a great sound mix. To run von Trier's grief-soaked, deliberately provocative, possibly misogyny-baiting shocker as a clean seminar in speculative realism — the recent movement that tries to think a world existing independent of any mind — is itself a *framing* — we do to *Antichrist* exactly what the husband does to the forest: we make it usable, orderable, ours. That's the joke we keep. *Antichrist* might just be a howl — a man wrecked by grief making the most upsetting object he could. "Nature Without Us" wins tonight's tournament. It does not win forever. It only stays champion until a reading walks in that can cross even the scenes *it* had to look away from.
