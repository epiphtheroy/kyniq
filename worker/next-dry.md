# Watch next — DRY (8 films)

## Black Swan (2010) — Darren Aronofsky
1. **The Red Shoes** (1948) · Michael Powell, Emeric Pressburger  
   Aronofsky's direct intertextual template: art as fatal possession, the male auteur extracting genius through a woman's destruction.
2. **Repulsion** (1965) · Roman Polanski  
   Same handheld-body camera rendering female interiority as horror; the apartment-as-psyche mirrors Nina's pink bedroom womb.
3. **Persona** (1966) · Ingmar Bergman  
   The double as returning repressed self; identity dissolution between two women examined with equal formal severity.
4. **Perfect Blue** (1997) · Satoshi Kon  
   Animated precursor Aronofsky literally sampled: surveillance, the double, and the performer policed by her own projected image.
5. **Suspiria** (1977) · Dario Argento  
   The dance academy as panopticon-horror space; aesthetic surface weaponized against the female body seeking mastery.
6. **Whiplash** (2014) · Damien Chazelle  
   The auteur-teacher's coercive pedagogy reframed as liberation; perfection-as-self-annihilation in a different performance discipline.
7. **Possessor** (2020) · Brandon Cronenberg  
   Body-horror as epistemological rupture; the self erased from inside through externally-demanded performance, shot with kinetic physicality.
8. **Neon Demon** (2016) · Nicolas Winding Refn  
   The neoliberal subject who polices herself until consumed; beauty industries as predatory panopticon devouring female competitors.
9. **The Piano Teacher** (2001) · Michael Haneke  
   The blade as proof where testimony fails; repressed desire, maternal suffocation, and self-mutilation in a high-art institution.

## Drive My Car (2021) — Ryūsuke Hamaguchi
1. **Certified Copy** (2010) · Abbas Kiarostami  
   A couple performs roles that may be real — the rehearsal method dissolves the line between script and grief.
2. **Happy Hour** (2015) · Ryūsuke Hamaguchi  
   Hamaguchi's five-hour table-read method exposed raw: the unfinished utterance as the only honest language between women.
3. **Scenes from a Marriage** (1973) · Ingmar Bergman  
   The withheld word as moral failure across a long marriage — cowardice disguised as consideration, grief deferred into silence.
4. **Uncle Vanya** (1970) · Andrei Konchalovsky  
   The source Chekhov text filmed: the coward as truer moralist, desire as slow self-destruction, directly illuminating Kafuku's stage work.
5. **Yi Yi** (2000) · Edward Yang  
   A three-hour still life of buried catastrophe — the real landscape of Taiwan as wound, ordinary cowardice as moral reckoning.
6. **The Wailing** (2016) · Na Hong-jin  
   A Korean landscape as buried catastrophe where silence and the voiceless body outweigh every spoken explanation.
7. **In the Mood for Love** (2000) · Wong Kar-wai  
   The unconfronted infidelity mourned through pure formal repetition — desire named only by its refusal, slowness as ethical stance.
8. **Wings of Desire** (1987) · Wim Wenders  
   The recorder that holds what no person can — an eternal witness attuned to interior monologue, the flattened voice releasing feeling.
9. **The Remains of the Day** (1993) · James Ivory  
   The cowardice of the withheld word as lifelong moral failure — a man who never possessed what he could not mourn.

## Spirited Away (2001) — Hayao Miyazaki
1. **Hausu** (1977) · Nobuhiko Obayashi  
   A house that devours girls enacts the same logic of domestic space as predatory contract consuming the feminine body.
2. **Pan's Labyrinth** (2006) · Guillermo del Toro  
   A child navigates a mythic underworld where obedience and resistance are tests of selfhood under fascist erasure of identity.
3. **Alice** (1988) · Jan Švankmajer  
   Wonderland rendered as uncanny puppet taxidermy — Carroll's original folk terror of the vanished child made viscerally material.
4. **The Tale of Princess Kaguya** (2013) · Isao Takahata  
   Ghibli's own formal antithesis: watercolour dissolution against Miyazaki's saturated world-building, both locate the soul in what capitalism cannot name.
5. **Welfare** (1975) · Frederick Wiseman  
   The bureaucratic institution that renames and processes bodies — the bathhouse contract made documentary flesh.
6. **Cleo from 5 to 7** (1962) · Agnès Varda  
   Identity located entirely in the name one inhabits; a woman's selfhood held hostage by forces that would reduce her to a diagnosis.
7. **Belladonna of Sadness** (1973) · Eiichi Yamamoto  
   Japanese animation as psychedelic folk cosmology where the feminine body becomes the last refuge the market and church colonize.
8. **The Exterminating Angel** (1962) · Luis Buñuel  
   Bourgeois guests trapped by invisible contract in a space they cannot leave — the bathhouse's inexplicable captivity as surrealist capitalism.
9. **Fantastic Planet** (1973) · René Laloux  
   An alien cosmology built entirely from labour hierarchy and the erasure of the subordinate species' inner name and culture.

## No Country for Old Men (2007) — Joel Coen
1. **Blood Simple** (1984) · Joel Coen  
   The Coens' debut shares No Country's Texas noir fatalism and characters undone by incomplete knowledge of lethal consequences.
2. **There Will Be Blood** (2007) · Paul Thomas Anderson  
   Parallel American myth: a monstrous sovereign figure whose principled ruthlessness exposes capitalism as the hidden violent order.
3. **Sicario** (2015) · Denis Villeneuve  
   The drug war as hidden sovereign, rendered through landscape silence and institutional violence that exceeds any moral framework.
4. **The Proposition** (2005) · John Hillcoat  
   A dying frontier genre mourning its own obsolescence, scored through silence and landscape rather than generic convention.
5. **Badlands** (1973) · Terrence Malick  
   Landscape cinematography as moral register and a killer whose detachment from consequence anticipates Chigurh's categorical indifference.
6. **A Prophet** (2009) · Jacques Audiard  
   The symbolic order collapsing into pure predatory logic, where institutional structures reveal themselves as competing violent sovereignties.
7. **Bring Me the Head of Alfredo Garcia** (1974) · Sam Peckinpah  
   A career director's formal refusal and elegy for a West already dead, with violence stripped of any redemptive grammar.
8. **The Assassination of Jesse James by the Coward Robert Ford** (2007) · Andrew Dominik  
   The obsolescence of a mythic figure rendered through voiceover and vast silence, mourning cinema's own lost iconographies.
9. **Wise Blood** (1979) · John Huston  
   A figure of terrifying Kantian consistency pursuing a self-legislated moral law through a landscape that has outlasted meaning.

## In the Mood for Love (2000) — Wong Kar-wai
1. **2046** (2004) · Wong Kar-wai  
   Direct continuation tracing Chow Mo-wan's grief into a future where desire solidifies into obsessive fiction.
2. **Chungking Express** (1994) · Wong Kar-wai  
   Hong Kong filmed as emotional displacement; longing crystallised into objects and routines rather than confessions.
3. **Brief Encounter** (1945) · David Lean  
   The ur-text of desire refusing consummation; the maxim 'we will not be like them' spoken entirely through restraint.
4. **The Umbrellas of Cherbourg** (1964) · Jacques Demy  
   Love memorialised through saturated colour and costume; the season named only from its end structures both films identically.
5. **Letter from an Unknown Woman** (1948) · Max Ophüls  
   Desire as fidelity to an impossible maxim; the beloved never fully perceives the love that has organised an entire life.
6. **Certified Copy** (2010) · Abbas Kiarostami  
   Rehearsal standing in for living; a couple performs intimacy until the performance becomes indistinguishable from the real.
7. **A City of Sadness** (1989) · Hou Hsiao-hsien  
   Shanghainese refugee bourgeoisie in postwar diaspora; history as atmosphere pressing silently on domestic interiors.
8. **The Scent of Green Papaya** (1993) · Tran Anh Hung  
   A Southeast Asian past reconstructed entirely on a French studio stage; the city filmed in a stand-in city as formal premise.
9. **Hiroshima Mon Amour** (1959) · Alain Resnais  
   The unspoken word that anchors desire; memory as architecture of grief where the past inhabits the present body.

## The Terminator (1984) — James Cameron
1. **Blade Runner** (1982) · Ridley Scott  
   Body-machine boundary dissolves identically; both films ask whether flesh authenticates personhood.
2. **La Jetée** (1962) · Chris Marker  
   The photograph as buried first cause: a single image seeds an inescapable causal loop.
3. **RoboCop** (1987) · Paul Verhoeven  
   Capital made flesh; the corporation owns the cyborg body, literalizing what Cameron implies.
4. **Predestination** (2014) · Michael Spierig  
   Pushes Cameron's predestination paradox to its purest, most vertiginous logical extreme.
5. **Possessor** (2020) · Brandon Cronenberg  
   The organic-mechanical border dissolves through corporate assassination; body is colonized hardware.
6. **Hardware** (1990) · Richard Stanley  
   Poverty-budget synth score and unstoppable killing machine in claustrophobic urban ruin; direct formal echo.
7. **The Hidden** (1987) · Jack Sholder  
   An implacable, body-hopping killer that cannot be reasoned with; Reese-figure pursues across L.A. streets.
8. **Westworld** (1973) · Michael Crichton  
   The machine that learns to govern turns lethal; Schwarzenegger's T-800 inherits this android's murderous logic.
9. **Alita: Battle Angel** (2019) · Robert Rodriguez  
   Cameron's own screenplay; reproductive futurism and cyborg identity revisited with his evolved digital threshold.
