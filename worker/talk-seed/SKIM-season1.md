## Day 1 — 이 거리들은 진짜다 — 개장 (45편)


**Aniki Bobo (1942)**

> **Gazette** — Denied a studio, Oliveira moved the production onto the streets of Porto and shot the city as it stood: riverfront stairs, shopfronts, the Douro traffic passing behind the boys. The constraint became the method — real light, real streets, children who lived there. Italy arrived at the same contract a year later and eventually gave it a name.


**Ossessione (1943)**

> **Gazette** — Wartime Italian screens ran to drawing rooms and white telephones. Visconti went to the Po valley instead and shot roadside inns, flat farmland, and people with nothing, undressed. The Fascist censors moved against the film; the method outlived them and came back after the war under the name neorealism.


**Bicycle Thieves (1948)**

> **Gazette** — The lead was a factory worker and the boy was found on the street. American money was available on the condition that a Hollywood star play the father; De Sica kept the worker and shot in a Rome where a bicycle was the difference between a wage and none. The picture went out to a country that recognized its own pavement.

> **Prism** — That refusal is the whole film in miniature — a star would have made the loss survivable, because stars survive. Casting a man who actually needed the job is what turns the last scene into a verdict instead of a plot point.


**Los Olvidados (1950)**

> **Gazette** — Before shooting, Buñuel walked the tenement districts on the edge of Mexico City and cast children out of the neighborhoods he had been watching. Mexico took the picture as an insult and pulled it from cinemas within days of the opening. Cannes reversed the verdict the following year, and it came home a classic.


**Forbidden Games (1952)**

> **Gazette** — It opens on the 1940 exodus — a road south, families on foot, aircraft coming in low — and then hands itself over to two children in a farmhouse. Clément shot their games at their own pace, without the polish French cinema usually applied to child performers. What the war does offscreen, the children rehearse in a barn, burying animals and stealing crosses for the graves.


**Summer with Monika (1953)**

> **Gazette** — The archipelago summer was the schedule and the subject both. Bergman worked with a small crew on available light — skin, water, weather, nothing hauled in to flatter anyone — and let the season end inside the film, returning the couple to a city apartment and a wage. Distributors abroad recut it and sold it as smut.

> **Prism** — The swimming was never the transgression; the transgression is the second half, where summer ends and someone has to make rent. Sold as a bad-girl picture, it is really an account of what freedom costs when only one person keeps paying for it.


**Pather Panchali (1955)**

> **Gazette** — There was no studio and no reliable money. Ray shot on weekends over several years in a village outside Calcutta, stopping each time the funds ran out, with a cameraman who had never shot a film and a cast that had mostly never acted. The West Bengal government paid to finish it, and the finished thing went around the world.


**Aparajito (1956)**

> **Gazette** — For the second Apu film Ray took his camera onto the ghats at Benares and shot the pilgrims, the barbers, and the wrestlers as they were, then followed the boy back to a Bengal village and on to a Calcutta printing press. Bengali audiences preferred the first film; Venice gave this one its top prize. The method had not changed — location, ordinary faces, the ordinary duration of a childhood ending.


**Sweet Smell of Success (1957)**

> **Gazette** — Broadway after dark, in real traffic outside real clubs: James Wong Howe shot the picture where the trade it depicts was still being conducted nightly. Planting items, trading girls for paragraphs, keeping a columnist fed — the press agent's work is rendered as an ordinary job with ordinary hours. Audiences who bought tickets for Lancaster and Curtis got a portrait of the men selling them their gossip, and stayed away.

> **Prism** — It failed commercially because it named the machine its own audience was plugged into, a column that could make or unmake a person by lunchtime. The city looks magnificent in Howe's night photography, which is the trick — the glamour is the product, and the deal always happens just outside the light.


**Cairo Station (1958)**

> **Gazette** — The terminal kept working while the picture was shot inside it: porters, luggage carts, the hawkers who slept on the platforms. Chahine cut real station footage into a drama about the men who lived off the trains, and played the lame news vendor himself. Egyptian audiences rejected it on release, and it sat for years before Europe called it a landmark.


**The World of Apu (1959)**

> **Gazette** — Apu is in a rented room over the Calcutta rail line, behind on rent, with a novel nobody has bought. Ray staged the wedding in a river village reached by boat and kept the ceremony's actual rhythm rather than a compressed version of it. Both leads were new to film, and neither performance looks like acting.


**Rocco and His Brothers (1960)**

> **Gazette** — A night train out of Lucania, a basement flat in Milan: Visconti set the film in the years when the North was absorbing southern migrants by the trainload into its factories and cheap rooms. He shot the city those migrants actually got — the periphery, the boxing gyms, the fog off the water at the Idroscalo. The economic miracle appears only as something happening to other people.

> **Prism** — The family doesn't fail to assimilate; it gets precisely what the boom was offering, which was piecework, a basement, and a way out through the ring. Milan's prosperity in those years ran on people it housed badly and never counted as its own.


**The Naked Island (1960)**

> **Gazette** — No one in the film speaks. Shindo took a crew small enough to feed on site out to a bare island in the Seto Inland Sea and filmed one family hauling fresh water up the terraces by shoulder pole, trip after trip, season after season. The company behind it was near collapse; the grand prize at Moscow kept the doors open.


**Accattone (1961)**

> **Gazette** — Pasolini had been writing about the Roman borgate for years before he directed anything; for his first film he went back to the same outskirts and cast the people he had been writing about, Franco Citti among them. The pimps, the dust, the half-built blocks at the edge of the city are locations, not sets dressed to resemble them. Over that he laid Bach, the only thing in the film that did not come from the neighborhood.


**Aimless Bullet (1961)**

> **Gazette** — The city was already the set. Yu shot in the ruins and refugee shanties still standing in Seoul eight years after the armistice, so nothing had to be built and nothing had to be faked. Within months of release the new military government pulled the film from cinemas, and it stayed off Korean screens for years.

> **Prism** — A film gets banned for showing rubble only when the rubble is still there and the state would rather it weren't. The realism contract cut both ways here: nothing had to be constructed, and nothing could be denied.


**Mamma Roma (1962)**

> **Gazette** — Pasolini built the film out of the borgate themselves — the postwar blocks thrown up on Rome's outer edge — and filled the frame with people who lived there rather than actors who could imitate them. The method came straight from the neorealist decade before it: real streets, real faces, a subproletariat Italian cinema had only recently learned to look at directly. What the camera records is a place, not a set dressed to resemble one.


**Black Girl (1966)**

> **Gazette** — Sembène shot on both ends of the crossing, Dakar and Antibes, in real apartments and real streets, and cast a lead with no acting career behind her. The subject is domestic service: a Senegalese woman brought to the Riviera to work in a white family's flat, six years after independence. African cinema had almost no feature-length precedent to lean on, and this one arrived with its realism already staked.


**Mouchette (1967)**

> **Gazette** — Bresson did not call them actors. He called them models, cast them without professional experience, and drilled the expression out of their line readings until what remained was flat and exact. Music enters the film barely at all; the realism here is enforced by subtraction, inside the same social-realist lineage the neorealists opened.

> **Prism** — Strip the performance out and nothing stands between the viewer and the event — no actor's grief absorbing it on your behalf. That's the cost of the method: by the end there is no performance left to hide behind.


**The Cow (1969)**

> **Gazette** — Mehrjui took the production into a real village and shot rural life at its own speed — subsistence, superstition, a man's whole livelihood standing in a single animal. The film is generally marked as the start of the Iranian New Wave, and its argument was made in method before theme: the village as location, not backdrop. Very little of the poverty on screen had to be constructed.


**Kes (1970)**

> **Gazette** — The cast came out of Barnsley — a local schoolboy in the lead, the rest drawn from the same coalfield — and Loach shot in the actual schools and streets. The Yorkshire speech was left uncorrected, thick enough that distributors abroad worried audiences would not follow it. The documentary habits show in the shooting itself: long lenses, real classrooms, a boy and a kestrel.


**Stroszek (1977)**

> **Gazette** — Herzog wrote the part for Bruno S. and then largely let him be himself in it, a Berlin street musician playing a Berlin street musician. The American stretch was filmed in rural Wisconsin with locals appearing as versions of themselves, in their own auction yards and bars, much of it unscripted. The result sits closer to documentation than to performance.

> **Prism** — Casting someone as a near-version of himself buys authenticity no director can manufacture, and it puts a real life on screen without the cover a character provides. Bruno S. carries the film and pays for it in the same frames.


**Where Is the Friend's House? (1987)**

> **Gazette** — The lead is a child with no training, and Kiarostami simply lets him walk — up the zigzag hill, back down, across the village, in takes that refuse to shorten the distance. Tedium is part of the contract: an errand this small only registers if the film gives it real time. Iranian neorealism starts less from subject matter than from that patience.


**Salaam Bombay! (1988)**

> **Gazette** — Nair recruited children already living on Bombay's streets, ran them through a workshop before shooting began, and then filmed in the districts they came from. The camera works out in the actual traffic of the markets, pavements and red-light lanes, at documentary distance. The verité was not a style applied later; it was settled at casting.


**The Story of Qiu Ju (1992)**

> **Gazette** — Zhang hid cameras in the streets and markets of rural Shaanxi and let scenes play out among people who did not know a film was running. Non-professionals fill most of the roles, and the locations are the ones the story concerns — village, township office, county court. The bureaucratic runaround has the texture of the real thing because nearly everything around it is.

> **Prism** — Hidden cameras buy an authenticity that consent would have spoiled; the passersby read as real precisely because nobody asked them. Part of the film's realism is borrowed from people who never agreed to lend it.


**The Fugitive (1993)**

> **Gazette** — The production worked in Chicago itself, filming the St. Patrick's Day parade during the actual parade and closing streets around a script still being rewritten between setups. The train wreck was staged practically, on real track, and the crew shot what happened. Much of what reads as documentary is a screenplay that had not yet caught up with its locations.


**La Promesse (1996)**

> **Gazette** — The Dardennes came out of documentary and kept the habits: handheld camera, faces without screen history, and Seraing — their own post-industrial town — standing for itself. Undocumented labour and a father's small business are filmed where such arrangements actually occur, at working distance. No shot in the film asks to be read as anything but Belgium.


**Children of Heaven (1997)**

> **Gazette** — Majidi filmed in the alleys of south Tehran, narrow enough that the camera has to run alongside the children rather than watch from somewhere comfortable. The plot turns on one lost pair of shoes, poverty measured in an object instead of a speech. The realism lives in the scale of the stake.

> **Prism** — A film about child poverty that never raises its voice loads everything onto the object: one pair of shoes has to carry what an argument would. It works, and it also lets an audience leave feeling warm about something that isn't.


**Taste of Cherry (1997)**

> **Gazette** — Kiarostami cast non-professionals and then kept them apart — the two men talking in the car were often filmed separately, each addressing the director sitting off-camera in the other seat. The conversations still hold, which is the method's claim: the realism is assembled shot by shot rather than performed. The dust roads in the hills outside Tehran do the rest.


**Central Station (1998)**

> **Gazette** — Salles found his young lead shining shoes at a Rio airport and put him opposite one of Brazil's most experienced actresses, then shot in the station the film is named for and on the roads running north from it. The crowds in frame are crowds, not extras arranged to resemble them. Brazilian street life supplies the material, not the décor.


**Not One Less (1999)**

> **Gazette** — The villagers play themselves here — a schoolgirl standing in as the substitute teacher, pupils as pupils, shopkeepers behind their own counters — and the school is the kind of underfunded rural classroom the story is about. None of the principal cast had acted before. The docu-fiction mode carries the argument: the room is not a reconstruction.

> **Prism** — When people play themselves, the film gets to enter their lives as evidence, and the shortage it documents becomes a story told in a register the authorities can live with. Both of those things are true of the same footage.


**The Wind Will Carry Us (1999)**

> **Gazette** — Kiarostami took his crew to a real Kurdish village and cast the people who lived there, playing versions of themselves on their own ground. The camera holds at a distance and lets scenes run well past the point where a fiction would cut. What the film calls a story is mostly the village continuing.


**A Time for Drunken Horses (2000)**

> **Gazette** — The mountain crossings between Iran and Iraq are a working economy, and Ghobadi filmed in a border village that lives off them, casting the people who do the work. The title names the practice: mules given alcohol to keep moving through the snow. Nobody in the cast was an actor.


**Beijing Bicycle (2001)**

> **Gazette** — Wang Xiaoshuai filmed on Beijing streets at the turn of the 2000s, while the hutong courtyards in his frames were being cleared block by block. The bicycle belongs to a courier newly arrived from the countryside, one of the migrant workers the city ran on and did not count. Sixth Generation directors worked this way by necessity: real locations, ordinary labor, no studio gloss.

> **Prism** — The demolition behind the actors wasn't set dressing, and it didn't pause for the shoot. Whatever the location footage caught is now the only version of those streets that exists.


**What Time Is It There? (2001)**

> **Gazette** — The camera stays put. Tsai films rooms in Taipei as rooms — real apartments, real stairwells, ambient noise where a score would go — and holds each shot for the length of the action rather than the length of a beat. The fiction agrees to run at the same speed as the day it depicts.


**The Son (2002)**

> **Gazette** — The Dardennes shoot in the industrial towns around Liège, in real workshops, with a handheld camera that stays close behind the back of a man's neck. Carpentry is filmed as work — measuring, planing, hauling lumber — and the drama arrives inside it rather than on top of it. No score cues the turn.


**Unknown Pleasures (2002)**

> **Gazette** — Jia Zhangke took a digital camera to Datong, a declining coal city in Shanxi, and cast people who were already there. On the radios and televisions at the edge of the frame in 2001: China's entry into the WTO, Beijing's winning bid for the 2008 Olympics. The teenagers in the foreground have nothing to do all day.

> **Prism** — The national good news plays at the edge of every shot and never reaches the people standing in it. That gap is the whole subject, and the documentary method is what makes it impossible to argue with.


**Turtles Can Fly (2004)**

> **Gazette** — Ghobadi cast local Kurdish children on the Iraq–Turkey border, among them refugees and kids maimed by landmines. The trade the film shows — digging mines out of the ground and selling them on — was ordinary work in that borderland, not invention. The injuries did not have to be designed by anyone.


**Colossal Youth (2006)**

> **Gazette** — Fontainhas, a Lisbon neighborhood built by Cape Verdean immigrants, was being demolished while Costa filmed its residents there as themselves. Ventura, who came to Portugal for construction work, carries the film under his own name, moving between the last standing rooms and the white rehousing blocks. The camera is set down and left there; nothing is restaged for it.


**Weekend (2011)**

> **Gazette** — Haigh shot in Nottingham with a small crew, in a real tower-block flat, letting scenes run long on a handheld camera. The two men talk the way people talk when no recorder is visible — overlapping, retracting, changing the subject. Two days, one flat, and no scene that announces itself as the important one.

> **Prism** — Realism here is a limit on scale, not just a look: the film gets only what a weekend can hold. Everything that would usually be a third act happens off-screen, later, to people the camera doesn't follow.


**The Bling Ring (2013)**

> **Gazette** — Between 2008 and 2009, a group of Los Angeles teenagers walked into the homes of Paris Hilton, Lindsay Lohan and other celebrities and carried out clothes, watches and cash, often through unlocked doors. Nancy Jo Sales reported the case for Vanity Fair, and Coppola built the film on that reporting, shooting in the Hollywood Hills where it happened. Hilton let the production film inside her actual house.


**Masaan (2015)**

> **Gazette** — Varanasi's burning ghats are an industry, staffed by hereditary labor held at the bottom of the caste order. Ghaywan filmed in that city and built the story out of it: a young man from the cremation trade, a young woman under police pressure, no villains anywhere. The river runs through both plots and registers neither.


**Capernaum (2018)**

> **Gazette** — Labaki cast the film out of Beirut's poorest streets; the boy at its center was a Syrian refugee living there, and the others stood close to the lives they play. The shoot ran long and loose, following children through real neighborhoods rather than sets. The court case the film is built around is a fiction, and almost nothing surrounding it is.

> **Prism** — Casting people to perform their own circumstances buys an authenticity nothing else can, and they are the ones who pay for it. The camera leaves; the neighborhood stays exactly where it was.


**Red Rocket (2021)**

> **Gazette** — Texas City is a refinery town, and Baker shot there in the summer of 2020 with a crew small enough not to close it down. Most of the faces around the lead were found locally and had never acted before. The refinery in the background is running; the film simply pointed at it.


**No Bears (2022)**

> **Gazette** — Panahi, barred from making films in Iran, plays a director working from a village near the Turkish border, running a shoot he cannot legally attend. The border in the film is a real border, and the crossing it circles is the kind people were making at the time. He was arrested in July 2022; the film premiered in Venice that September without him.


**Vermiglio (2024)**

> **Gazette** — Delpero named the film after Vermiglio, an actual village in the Trentino Alps, and shot there in the local dialect. The story sits in the last winter of the Second World War, when the war reached that valley mainly as absent men and arriving strangers. Chores, seasons and Mass set the pace; the history arrives as weather.

> **Prism** — Naming a real village makes the film answerable to it — the dialect, the shape of the year, what a household actually did in February. That's a heavier obligation than period costume, and the film takes it on.


## Day 2 — 신문이 먼저 알았다 — 실화 취재 (15편)


**Battleship Potemkin (1925)**

> **Gazette** — The mutiny is real: June 1905, the crew of the Potemkin refusing maggoty meat off Odessa, officers thrown overboard. Odessa saw killing that week; the staircase did not. The Primorsky Stairs have been known as the Potemkin Steps ever since — a landmark named for something that happened only on film.


**On the Waterfront (1954)**

> **Gazette** — Budd Schulberg built the script out of Malcolm Johnson's New York Sun series on racketeering along the New York docks, reporting that took the Pulitzer in 1949. Kazan shot on the Hoboken piers through the winter, with working longshoremen filling the crowds. The shape-up staged in the film was a hiring system, not a plot device.


**Death by Hanging (1968)**

> **Gazette** — Ri Chin'u, a Zainichi Korean teenager in Tokyo, was convicted in the 1958 Komatsugawa Incident of killing two schoolgirls and hanged in 1962, at twenty-two. Ōshima opens not on drama but on procedure — the chamber, the trapdoor, the officials and their assigned roles, itemized the way the state itemizes them. Japan hangs its condemned by that same method today.

> **Prism** — Getting the procedure exactly right is the whole maneuver: once the gallows works precisely as the manual says, whatever goes wrong afterward can't be filed under sloppiness. The state is left no room to call it an aberration.

## Day 3 — 재현의 장인들 (30편)


**Master of the House (1925)**

> **Gazette** — Dreyer had a life-size apartment built inside the studio rather than assembled from flats — rooms in true proportion, doors that led somewhere, a kitchen that functioned. The camera could cross a threshold instead of cutting around it. The domestic tyranny in the film is measured out in floor plan.


**The General (1926)**

> **Gazette** — Keaton ran working locomotives on narrow-gauge track in Oregon, and staged the bridge collapse by taking a trestle out from under a moving engine. There was one take, because there was one engine. It lay in the riverbed for years afterward, until wartime scrap crews came for it.


**Meet Me in St. Louis (1944)**

> **Gazette** — A row of houses went up on the Culver City backlot and stood in for a Missouri that had been gone forty years. The Louisiana Purchase Exposition itself was built to be temporary — staff and plaster, demolished within months of closing in 1904 — and was raised again in paint for the finale. The street outlasted the picture on the lot.

> **Prism** — A fair designed to disappear got its permanent version from a studio. For a lot of people the 1904 Exposition now looks the way MGM decided it looked.

## Day 4 — 역사의 현장에서 찍다 (12편)


**There Was a Father (1942)**

> **Gazette** — Ozu released this in 1942, with Japan five years into war in China and a year into the Pacific, and every script passing a government reader before a camera turned. Onscreen there is no war: a widowed schoolteacher resigns after a student drowns on a school outing, takes work in Tokyo, and sees his son a few days a year. The version that survives is a shortened postwar print — some of what one regime's censors approved, the next regime's censors cut.


**The Best Years of Our Lives (1946)**

> **Gazette** — Harold Russell was not an actor. He had lost both hands in a stateside training accident and was appearing in an Army rehabilitation short when Wyler cast him as Homer — hooks, and no double. The picture opened in 1946 with the country still absorbing its returning armies, and it put the paperwork of readjustment onscreen: prosthetics, bank loans, marriages that had aged three years in absence.


**Late Spring (1949)**

> **Gazette** — Japan in 1949 was still an occupied country, and Ozu made a film in which the only crisis is a marriage. Noriko is twenty-seven, keeping house in Kamakura for her widowed father, and the family's real business is arranging a match before the age passes. The camera sits at floor height and does not move; the pressure of the period arrives entirely through who pours whose tea and who is told which lie.

> **Prism** — The realism contract cuts both ways here. Filming the arrangement as routine makes it look like weather instead of a decision someone made — and the daughter's wedding smile and the father alone with an apple afterward are the same event, shot from the side that didn't have to choose.

## Day 5 — 배우가 아닌 사람들 (20편)


**Pickpocket (1959)**

> **Gazette** — Bresson brought in Kassagi, a professional sleight-of-hand artist, to choreograph the thefts, then cast him in the film as one of the accomplices. The hands carry the performance: wallets lifted at the Gare de Lyon in a relay, a watch off a wrist, a jacket held open a half-second too long. Nothing is hidden in a cutaway, and the technique is legible enough that the film has been called, not entirely as a joke, a manual.


**Vagabond (1985)**

> **Gazette** — Varda shot in the Languedoc in winter and filled the frame with people who lived there — vine-pruners, a garage mechanic, a goatherd with a philosophy degree — many of them non-professionals speaking close to their own lives. The film opens with Mona dead in a ditch and reconstructs her backward out of what strangers remember, which is mostly what she cost them. The camera keeps sliding leftward across the landscape and letting her walk out of the frame.


**Dust in the Wind (1986)**

> **Gazette** — The script came out of Wu Nien-jen's own youth in the coal country above Keelung, and Hou filmed it where it happened: the mine mouths, the single-track railway, the town whose only future was in Taipei. A boy and a girl go down to the city for printing and tailoring work, and the film holds still while the years take them. He is sent off to military service; she marries the postman who had been delivering his letters.

> **Prism** — The long takes aren't patience for its own sake — they're the exchange rate. Nothing dramatic takes the girl away, only the ordinary arithmetic of a country moving off the mountain, and the camera stays wide enough to show that nobody decided it.

## Day 6 — 권력의 기록 (15편)


**The Passion of Joan of Arc (1928)**

> **Gazette** — Dreyer worked from the surviving minutes of the 1431 condemnation trial and put the judges' recorded questions back in their mouths. Months of interrogation were compressed into a single day, but the words are the record's words. The faces were shot without makeup and in close-up, so the transcript arrives attached to a body.

> **Prism** — The catch is whose paper it is. Those minutes were kept by the court that burned her, so the most faithful source available is also the prosecution's version, and every line of Joan's comes through the men transcribing her.


**Le Corbeau (1943)**

> **Gazette** — The letters came first from Tulle, where between 1917 and 1922 an anonymous hand mailed accusations through the town until it turned on itself. Clouzot restaged the phenomenon in 1943, financed by Continental, the German-owned studio operating in occupied Paris. At the Liberation the film was suppressed and Clouzot was banned from directing; the ban lasted two years.


**The Last Emperor (1987)**

> **Gazette** — The script follows Puyi's own memoir, written after years in the Fushun re-education prison — which is to say the record it engages was itself produced under supervision. Bertolucci was granted permission to shoot inside the Forbidden City, the first Western production allowed in. A man crowned there as a toddler ended his life tending plants in a Beijing garden, and the film keeps both facts in the same frame.

## Day 7 — 몸으로 찍은 영화 (12편)


**Breathless (1960)**

> **Gazette** — Coutard carried the camera through actual Paris streets in 1959, working in available light among passersby nobody had hired. Godard handed his actors lines the morning they were shot, and the jump cuts arrived later, in an edit that had to shorten the film without dropping whole scenes. The New Wave's claim to have filmed life as found has been checked against that shooting record ever since.

> **Prism** — Shooting in the street doesn't guarantee honesty; it just moves the staging somewhere harder to see. Half the spontaneity people credit to Breathless was decided at a cutting bench weeks later, and a doctrine got built on top of it anyway.


**A Woman Under the Influence (1974)**

> **Gazette** — No studio would finance it, so Cassavetes and Peter Falk paid for the picture out of their own pockets, borrowing against property to keep the shoot alive. The camera stayed handheld inside a real house, and scenes were allowed to run well past their usable length. When it was finished, Cassavetes distributed it himself, booking theaters and campuses one at a time.
