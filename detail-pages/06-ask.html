AnsweringHow does cinema portray surveillance?sample

On Metatake, surveillance is rarely a matter of cameras and wiretaps — it surfaces first as the gaze, the question of who is permitted to watch whom. Dreyer's The Passion of Joan of Arc keeps cutting to the faces of the watching crowd until the spectator is folded into it: your own looking becomes part of the execution [1]. Read in a colder register, the same scene is a broadcast — a public burning staged so the onlookers will learn what happens to those who refuse to conform, power performing itself for an audience [2].

The watching eye is also an instrument of authority. In Modern Times, Chaplin puts the boss's face on a screen-within-the-screen, looming over the shop floor; composition alone tells you who owns the building, decades before the workplace camera was ordinary [3]. And cinema is uneasily aware that it is itself a watching machine: A Matter of Life and Death sets a camera obscura at its heart — a "gentle, loving surveillance," the apparatus of the movies quietly watching life and telling stories about what it sees [4].

Pulled together, these readings suggest cinema portrays surveillance as a moral relation rather than a technology: to watch is to hold power, to implicate, and sometimes to care — and the screen keeps turning that watching back on the person in the seat.
