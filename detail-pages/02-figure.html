The scholar's rock (Suseok)

a figure inParasite (2019)dir. Bong Joon Ho
Kind Trope Type The Household Thing Made Deadly Takes 4

In Parasite, Min gifts the Kims a suseok mounted on a wooden stand, calling it metaphorical. Ki-woo carries it through the film, takes it into the flooded basement, and later uses it in the bunker, where Geun-sae strikes him with it.

Takes

A take is one critical reading of this figure. Takes that recur across films converge into a meta take — the hub each one links to. Filed by critical register.

Existential→The Last Stubborn No

Ki-woo says the thing won't leave him alone, and he's right in a way he doesn't grasp. He clutches the rock as a promise that his life will rise above the basement, and in the flood it absurdly bobs on the water like hope itself refusing to sink. Then it comes back to fracture his head. The object he carried as a ladder out turns into the weight that nearly kills him — aspiration revealed as the exact stone you drown holding.

Mythic→The Talisman's Backfire (emerging)

It arrives like a charmed heirloom, passed from a wealthier friend with the air of blessing — a stone that supposedly draws wealth to a household. But every gift that promises fortune in stories carries a price, and this one delivers flood and blood instead of luck. The Kims treat it as a talisman and it behaves like a curse, the kind of object that grants its wish in the cruelest literal form. Ki-woo only escapes once he finally puts it back in the stream.

Philosophical→Land And Heritage Extracted

Min insists the rock is symbolic, and Ki-woo nods as if meaning alone could feed a family. The whole point becomes that you can't eat a metaphor. When the water rises and the violence starts, the suseok stops being an idea and reverts to plain mass — heavy, indifferent, capable of cracking bone. The film keeps testing the gap between the beautiful concept poor people are handed and the brute material fact they're left holding.

SemioticCommunity→Desire Conferred By Distance

The scholar's rock, gifted to the Kims, functions as a symbol of abstract aspirational wealth, illustrating Commodity Fetishism. The Kims imbue it with mystical power for social mobility, fetishizing it as an object that can transcend their material conditions. In reality, it is just a heavy, burdensome stone, mirroring the weight of their false consciousness and the impracticality of their hopes for easy advancement.
