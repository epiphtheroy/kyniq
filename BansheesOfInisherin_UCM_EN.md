# ULTRA CREATIVE MISREADING № 4
### *The Banshees of Inisherin*, dir. Martin McDonagh (2022)

## Tonight's Card

A man who plays the fiddle decides he no longer wishes to speak to his only friend. To make the point stick, he promises that every time the friend opens his mouth, he will cut off one of his own fingers. Then he does it — finger after finger, until the whole hand that made his music is gone, flung at the friend's door. One of those fingers ends up choking a beloved donkey to death. Across the water, a civil war rumbles on, heard but never seen. Three readings have walked in tonight to fight over what all this means. Two are beautiful. Two will lose. The prize is the usual one: only the reading that can cross the entire film without once looking away gets to keep the belt.

## The Rules

A *creative misreading* is a bold, theory-armed interpretation that deliberately shoves a film past its polite meaning. The question is never "what did McDonagh intend." The question is "what is the most powerful machine this film will actually run." All three contenders tonight are strong — weak readings don't get a walk-out. But the tournament has one law, and it is merciless: **a reading stays alive only as long as it can cross every major scene without flinching.** The moment it has to skip a scene, or wave it off as "just atmosphere," or quietly change the subject — that's the fold. It's out.

## Contender I — "Friend Becomes Enemy"

**The walk-out.** This one owns the war. All through the film, artillery thuds in from the mainland — gunfire, a distant column of smoke, the year is 1923 and Ireland is tearing itself in half. And here on the little island of Inisherin, in miniature, two men who were inseparable yesterday are now sworn against each other. The feud *rhymes* with the war. Brother against brother out there; friend against friend in here. The reading practically draws itself: this is a film about the political, about the line men draw to turn a neighbor into an opponent.

**Concept Card.** Carl Schmitt, in *The Concept of the Political* (1932), argued that politics has one founding act and one only: the drawing of the *friend/enemy distinction*. Not enemy as someone you hate personally — enemy as the public other you set yourself against, the line that says *us* on this side and *them* on that. Schmitt thought every real political moment comes down to this cut; a community exists by deciding who its enemy is. Bring that to Inisherin and the case lights up: the Civil War is friend/enemy at the scale of a nation, and Colm-versus-Pádraic is the same cut at the scale of two stools in a pub.

**The case.** It's a genuinely thrilling fit, and the film hands you the parallel on a plate. The war is never shown, only heard — which lets the feud become its visible face, the cut you can actually watch happen. Read this way, the film is about how a bond becomes a border, how the most ordinary friendship carries the seed of the line that splits a country. For a good while, no reading looks more obviously right.

**The fold.** It flinches at the threat itself — the shears. Watch what Colm actually says: every time Pádraic talks to *him*, *he* will cut off one of *his own* fingers. An enemy aims outward; you draw a line so you can stand against someone. Colm draws no line against Pádraic. He doesn't want Pádraic as his enemy — he wants him as *nothing*, no bond at all, not even the bond of hostility. An enemy is still a relation; you are joined to the person you fight. Colm is trying to leave the field entirely, and the proof is that the blade points at his own hand, not at Pádraic. Schmitt has a rich grammar for turning a friend into a foe. He has none for the man who refuses both — who wants out of the friend/enemy game altogether. The shears are not a weapon against an enemy. They are a man amputating a relation.

**Verdict.** Eliminated, with respect. It mistook a withdrawal for a war.

## Contender II — "The Island as Nowhere"

**The walk-out.** This one owns the landscape. Inisherin is a fictional island off Ireland's west coast, and McDonagh shoots it as a mood: low stone walls, wind-flattened grass, grey water, a sky that presses down. Nothing on this island connects to anywhere; the mainland is a rumor across the channel. People walk the same paths to the same pub and the same shore, in weather that never lifts. The place doesn't feel like a setting so much as a *feeling* — bleakness made out of scenery. A reading is waiting right there: the meaning of the film is in the island, in its closed-off, going-nowhere emptiness.

**Concept Card.** Gilles Deleuze, writing about cinema (*Cinema 1*, 1983), named a certain kind of film-space the *any-space-whatever* — a space stripped of the usual job of telling you where you are or where to go next. It stops being a map and becomes pure mood: a place charged with feeling but disconnected, a setting that has come loose from cause and direction. Deleuze prized it because such a space stops serving the plot and starts radiating raw affect on its own. Lay that over Inisherin and the fit is immediate: the island as a disconnected, dread-soaked space whose whole point is the atmosphere it gives off.

**The case.** On paper it's seductive, because the film really is a triumph of place. The dread does live partly in the geography — the sense of an island you cannot get off, a closed loop of grey. Read this way, *Banshees* becomes a study in landscape-as-emotion, where the bleak, unconnected island *is* the despair, and you need no plot at all to feel it. The mood is genuinely in the stones.

**The fold.** It flinches at the shears and the door — the same place, oddly, where Contender I fell, but for the opposite reason. The horror of this film does not radiate from the scenery. It happens in a *deed*, in *time*: Colm raises the shears, cuts, and walks the severed finger across the island to fling it at a particular door, on a particular afternoon, because a particular sentence was spoken. The any-space-whatever reading takes a problem of *act and time* and relocates it into *space*. But move Colm and Pádraic to a sunlit city and the structure is unchanged — a man still destroys the hand that makes his music to chase a lasting work, and a finger still ends up in a donkey's throat. The wound was never in the landscape. The reading went looking for the void in the grey water when the void is in the structure of what Colm *does*. It found a beautiful island and missed the cut.

**Verdict.** Eliminated, with respect. It mistook the weather for the wound.

## The Final — "The Masterpiece That Eats the Hand"

Go back to the single most disturbing object in the film: a severed finger, traveling. From the shears, to the road, to Pádraic's door, and finally — impossibly — into the throat of a small donkey, where it lodges and kills. Hold that image. A finger that should have played music, now a piece of refuse that murders an innocent animal.

Now notice how both losers fell the same way. Contender I looked for the meaning in a *relation* — friend, enemy, the line between. Contender II looked for it in a *place* — the island, the grey, the mood. But the film's real engine isn't a relation and isn't a place. It's an *act with a self-cancelling shape*: the making of a Work. Colm is destroying the very hand that makes his music in the name of leaving something that lasts. The champion's whole move is to read the film as that one impossible deed.

**Concept Card I.** Georges Bataille, across the 1930s and in *The Accursed Share* (1949), broke with the idea that economies are only about scarcity and saving. Every system, he argued, builds up a surplus it cannot reinvest — an *accursed share* — and that excess has to be spent, burned off, given away in pure loss. He called this kind of spending *dépense*: expenditure with no return expected, the opposite of a careful trade. Sacrifice, festival, war, the grand pointless gift — these are *dépense*, a society discharging its surplus rather than hoarding it. Bring it to Inisherin and Pádraic's whole world stands revealed as the *opposite*: milk, pints, the small daily niceties, an economy of decent return where everything circulates and balances. Colm's cutting is *dépense* — a finger spent for nothing, no return possible.

**Concept Card II.** Maurice Blanchot, writing on literature (from the 1950s on), named a strange truth about art *désœuvrement* — "worklessness," or the unworking of the work. His point was that the genuine work of art never closes into a finished, owned thing; it carries inside it something that undoes it, an incompletion it can't shake off. The masterpiece, pursued hard enough, reveals itself as the *impossibility* of the masterpiece. That is exactly the tune Colm is chasing. He wants the imperishable composition, the thing that outlives him — and the harder he reaches for it, the more the film unworks it in his hands.

Now watch this reading cross the whole film, including both scenes that knocked the others out.

**The shears** — where Contender I flinched — are *dépense* made flesh. Colm severs the fiddler's own fingers: he destroys the instrument of music to consecrate the *idea* of music, the legacy, the remembered tune. This is sovereign spending in its purest form — he gives up the useful organ for a transcendence that the very act annihilates. You cannot compose with a ruined hand. He spends the means of the Work to honor the end of the Work, and gets, in return, nothing. Not an enemy, not a victory — pure loss. The blade points at his own hand because the logic is sacrificial, not political. He is burning his surplus, and the surplus is himself.

**The tune** — the composition Colm names "The Banshees of Inisherin" — is *désœuvrement* you can hear. We never get the finished piece. The music arrives only in fragments, interrupted, partial, a Work perpetually about to exist and never delivered. And here is the cruelty the other readings can't touch: the amputations run *against* the music — the cutting and the composing pull in opposite directions. Each finger lost subtracts from the hand that would author the tune, so the Work draws closer to its meaning exactly as the power to make it is destroyed. The masterpiece is approached by way of the impossibility of making it. The pursuit of the lasting work consummates itself as the inability to work at all.

**The distant war**, which Contender I leaned on so hard, finds its real place here too — not as a friend/enemy mirror, but as the same logic at the scale of history. The artillery is heard, never shown: a community unworking itself offscreen. The tune and the war share one shape — present as sound, absent as image, real only as the thing that won't resolve into form. The war doesn't end; it just "goes on." So does the feud.

**Jenny's throat** — the scene neither loser can survive — is where both halves of the reading detonate together. The severed finger is the *accursed share*: the surplus no economy of legacy can absorb. Colm meant it to circulate as a sign — the cost of his art, his terrible seriousness. But the film refuses to let it mean. It reverts to a brute object and lodges in the throat of Pádraic's beloved miniature donkey, the literal site of breath and life, and kills her. Expenditure devours the very life it claimed to rise above. There is no lesson in it, nothing to recover; the donkey's death is gratuitous on purpose. This is the moment the Work's pursuit of immortality produces its one real product: a needless death.

**The arson** closes the case by refusing to close. Pádraic burns down Colm's house with Colm inside — and Colm survives anyway; Pádraic had carried the dog to safety first, sparing the animal but not the man, yet Colm walks out of the fire alive. Even annihilation is *unworked*: the burning yields no death, no ending. The feud is preserved precisely in its irresolution, like the war that simply continues. (The confessional fits the same key: Colm brings not sin but *despair* — boredom, the dread of being forgotten — and the sacrament has nothing to operate on, because grace, like exchange, finds no purchase on a man who has stepped out of the economy entirely.) Nothing in this film resolves into a finished thing. That is the point.

This reading skips nothing. That's why it takes the belt.

## The Decision

Accept "The Masterpiece That Eats the Hand" and *Banshees* stops being a sad comedy about two Irishmen who fall out, and becomes something stranger: a demonstration that to make a lasting Work and to destroy yourself are not two steps but one. Colm wants to be remembered, to leave the imperishable tune. The film grants the wish and shows the bill — that the monument can only be paid for by spending the very hand that builds it, and that the monument, pursued to the end, reveals itself as worklessness. The proof of the void is a finger in a donkey's throat. Read this way, the film no longer asks whether kindness matters or art is worth it. It asks something colder: whether the thing you make to outlive you is, in its deepest structure, the thing that ends you.

## The Asterisk

And now the honest part, because this is a misreading too — gloriously, on purpose. *The Banshees of Inisherin* is, on its surface, a bleak and very funny film about two men, a pub, a donkey, and a stubbornness that gets out of hand. To run it as fundamental ontology — to read a fiddler's sulk as Bataille's solar economy of sacrifice, and a half-finished folk tune as Blanchot's unworking of the Work — is itself a willful, knowingly grandiose framing. McDonagh wrote dialogue you laugh at; we have walked in and called it a treatise on the void at the foundation of art. There is a real chance the film is mostly a tragicomedy about despair and male pride, and that the donkey is plain heartbreak, not a symbol of the void. "The Masterpiece That Eats the Hand" wins tonight's tournament. It does not win forever. It only stays champion until a reading walks in that can cross even the scenes *it* had to look away from.
