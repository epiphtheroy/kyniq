# ULTRA CREATIVE MISREADING № 3
### *The Bodyguard*, dir. Mick Jackson (1992)

## Tonight's Card

A pop star is being stalked. A grim ex-Secret Service man is hired to keep her alive. They fall in love. He takes a bullet for her at the Academy Awards, lives, and then they part — over one of the best-selling soundtracks in history and a woman singing "I Will Always Love You" loud enough to crack the room. Three readings have walked in tonight to fight over what this glossy thing actually *is*. Two of them are dazzling. Two of them will lose. The prize is the one it always is: the only reading allowed to win is the one that can walk through the entire film without once looking away.

## The Rules

A *creative misreading* is a bold, theory-armed interpretation that deliberately shoves a film past its polite meaning. The question is never "what did Mick Jackson intend." The question is "what is the most powerful machine I can build that this film will actually run." All three contenders tonight are strong — weak readings don't get a walk-out. But the tournament has one law and it is merciless: **a reading stays alive only as long as it can cross every major scene without flinching.** The instant it has to skip a scene, or wave it off as decoration, or quietly change the subject — that's the fold. It's out.

---

## Contender I — "Star as Pure Image"

**The walk-out.** This one owns the screens, and the film is *drowning* in them. Rachel Marron reaches us first as a face on a monitor, a poster on a wall, a name on a marquee — always mid-performance, mid-broadcast, mid-magazine-cover. The whole back third climbs toward the Academy Awards: a telecast beamed to the planet, a woman watched by more eyes at once than any body could ever feel. No reading looks more obviously correct. This is a film about a person who exists mainly as an image.

**Concept Card.** Its first engine is Guy Debord, whose *Society of the Spectacle* (1967) argues that in modern life, real relationships between people get replaced by relationships between *images* — "capital accumulated to the point where it becomes an image." He built the idea to explain how a whole society could come to live by watching rather than doing. Bolt on Jean Baudrillard's *hyperreal* (from *Simulacra and Simulation*, 1981): the copy with no original behind it, the image that points to no real thing, only to other images. Drop both into the film and Rachel writes herself — not a woman the cameras happen to film, but a being *made of* being filmed, a star with no flesh-and-blood original underneath.

**The case.** It's a thrilling fit, on the surface almost unbeatable. The film really does treat Rachel as a luminous commodity — packaged, projected, sold. Frank's first task is to watch *her watching herself* on playback. The Oscar sequence is spectacle in Debord's purest sense: millions in a relationship not with Rachel but with the broadcast of Rachel.

**The fold.** It flinches at the bullet. The climax turns on a real body stepping in front of a real round — Frank throwing his flesh between the gun and the star. "Star as Pure Image" talks endlessly *about* images, then goes mute the instant the film stops being about images and becomes about *meat*. A reading built on "there is no real thing under the picture" cannot survive a scene whose whole point is that there *is* one — a body that bleeds, that can be ended, that must be physically interposed to keep another body intact. To stay upright it has to treat the film's central act of violence as set-dressing. Its biggest moment becomes the reading's blind spot.

**Verdict.** Eliminated, with respect. It read every screen in the building and missed the one body bleeding on the floor.

## Contender II — "All About the Gaze"

**The walk-out.** This one owns the looking, and the film is built out of looking. Frank is a professional *watcher*: he maps sightlines, positions himself to see without being seen, scans crowds for the one face that doesn't belong. The house is a grid of mirrors and monitors. Half the movie is one person observing another — the bodyguard studying the star, the star catching the bodyguard at it, the stalker out in the dark studying them both. The film is one long architecture of who-sees-whom.

**Concept Card.** Its engine is Laura Mulvey, whose essay "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema" (1975) coined *to-be-looked-at-ness*: the way mainstream film positions women on screen to be looked at, frozen as spectacle for an assumed male eye, while men drive the action. She built it to expose how cinema's pleasure is wired around a watching man and a watched woman. Behind her stands Jacques Lacan and the *gaze* — the unnerving sense that you are not just the one looking but also the one *being looked at* by a field of sight you don't control. Put these on the film and the asymmetry snaps into focus: Rachel is the looked-at, Frank the looker, and desire is the current running between them.

**The case.** Textually it's strong, maybe the strongest on paper. The romance really does ignite through looking — the charged glances, the bodyguard who can't stop watching the body he's paid to watch, the star who learns to perform for the one man trying *not* to be her audience. As an account of how the movie generates desire, it's close to airtight.

**The fold.** It flinches at the interposition. Go to the Oscars, to the instant the shot is fired and Frank moves. Up to that second the reading was perfect — the whole film had been a story of eyes. But now the watcher does something the language of looking has no word for: he stops being an eye and becomes *shielding flesh*, a wall between the bullet and the star. A reading that lives in seeing and being-seen cannot say why the looker must turn into *cover* — why the scopic relation (who-watches-whom) suddenly collapses into a sacrificial one (whose-body-absorbs-the-blow). Sightlines explain the romance up to the gunshot and then go silent. The reading can describe everyone looking; it cannot describe the body that steps in front.

**Verdict.** Eliminated, with respect. It mapped every sightline and had nothing to say when a watcher turned himself into a shield.

## The Final — "The Body Bought to Be Killable"

Go back to two quiet beats most viewers forget. In one, Frank is watching an old movie at home: Kurosawa's *Yojimbo*, the masterless samurai who answers to no lord. In the other, Frank shows off his own sword — he lets a silk scarf fall onto the upturned blade, and it drifts down and parts in two halves. Hold that second image. A weightless thing meeting an edge, and the edge deciding, in an instant, life from death.

Now notice how both losers fell the same way. One stayed at the level of *images*, the other at the level of *looking* — both kept the film up on the screen, in the realm of pictures and glances. The champion's whole move is to climb down off the screen into the one thing the others couldn't touch: **mortal flesh, contracted and exchanged.**

**Concept Card I.** Giorgio Agamben, in *Homo Sacer* (1995), revives an obscure figure from ancient Roman law: the *homo sacer*, the "sacred man" who can be *killed* by anyone without it counting as murder, yet cannot be sacrificed in a ritual. He is life stripped of all protection — what Agamben calls *bare life* — a body the law has emptied of value while keeping alive. Agamben built the idea to show how modern power creates zones where normal law is switched off and some bodies become killable. The switch he calls the *sovereign exception*: the sovereign is whoever can suspend the law, declaring an emergency in which the ordinary rule "you may not kill this person" no longer holds. It belongs here because that is exactly what a bodyguard contract does — it pre-designates one body as the one allowed to die.

**Concept Card II.** René Girard, across *Violence and the Sacred* (1972) and *The Scapegoat* (1982), argues that communities discharge their built-up violence onto a single substitute victim — the *scapegoat* — whose death restores peace; and that the violence builds up through *mimetic rivalry*: we desire things because others desire them, and end up locked in jealous competition with our own doubles. Girard built this to explain why nearly every culture has a founding murder at its root. It's here because *The Bodyguard* runs on exactly one substitution — one body offered up so the rest of the system can be calm.

Now watch it walk the whole film, including both scenes that knocked the others out.

**The hiring** is not employment; it's consecration. Frank's austerity, his refusal of intimacy, his cold control of every door and sightline read as thriller texture, a man being aloof. Read as political theology, it's a body *stripping itself down* to enter the zone where it can be spent. He locks the house, rehearses the evacuation, draws the perimeter — and the perimeter is a map of the exception: a space where the rule that all lives weigh the same has been switched off, one life marked in advance as the one that may be lost. He is *paid to be killable* so Rachel may be made *unkillable*. The contract is a sacrifice with an invoice.

**The severed scarf** — Frank's own demonstration, rhymed with the *Yojimbo* he watches — is the film telling us its own logic. Kurosawa's masterless samurai is a sovereign with no master above him; he answers only to his own code. Frank is the same fold. He is *sovereign*, deciding the exception — who enters, when to run, whose body goes in front. And he is *homo sacer*, the one that very exception will consume. (Agamben holds these two apart as opposite poles; folding them into one disciplined body is the reading's deliberate gamble.) The blade cutting the weightless silk is the whole movie in one gesture: the razor-thin line between living and dying, decided by a body that has made itself the instrument of the cut.

**The Oscars** — where *both* losers fell — is the scapegoat mechanism performed live for the planet. The shot is aimed at the sovereign-image, the most-watched body on Earth that night; it lands in the substitute body instead. That is where Contender I had nothing to say, because a real round goes into real flesh; and where Contender II went silent, because the watcher stops being an eye and becomes the shield. This reading answers both at once: the looking was *always* in service of the substitution — Frank watched so that, at the threshold, he would know exactly where to put his body. And the cruel detail the reading *needs*: the broadcast does not stop. The telecast neither breaks down nor mourns; the broadcast swallows the substitution as one more event. The spectacle is real, but it works as a cover: it shows the sacrifice happening while hiding what the sacrifice actually is.

**Nicki, the sister** who secretly hired the killer and is herself killed, closes the case. Her envy of Rachel — coveting the stardom she can never embody, plotting against her own double — is Girard's mimetic rivalry exactly: desire caught on a rival you can't stop measuring yourself against, the violence rebounding onto its source. The system needs a death to re-found the difference between intimate and assassin, sister and rival. It supplies two bodies to drain the violence — Nicki's, then Frank's wound. Blood-bond and contract-bond end on the same substitutable flesh.

This reading skips nothing — screens, sightlines, scarf, bullet, sister, each one a single operation: one body rendered exchangeable for another. That's why it takes the belt.

## The Decision

Accept "The Body Bought to Be Killable" and *The Bodyguard* stops being a romance with a violent third act and becomes a liturgy wearing a love story as its coat. The romance isn't the content; it's the cover story over a transaction in mortality. Frank is not really a lover but a sacrificial operator — a man who contracts his own flesh to be killable so a star's flesh can be made safe, and the tenderness between them is what makes the trade bearable to watch. The most famous image in the film, the bodyguard carrying the star out of danger, stops being a romantic high point and becomes what it always was beneath the swelling music: the carrying of the sacrificial body from the altar. And notice the film's quiet honesty — Frank *lives*, and they *part*. The love never pays out in marriage, because the love was never the point. The transaction is complete; the bodies separate; the rite is done.

## The Asterisk

And now the honest part, because this is a misreading too — gloriously, on purpose. Agamben wrote *Homo Sacer* about concentration camps and the hidden logic of modern sovereignty; Girard wrote about founding murders at the root of culture. To run a Whitney Houston blockbuster — a film built to sell records and make grown adults cry in the dark — as a treatise in political theology is a willful, slightly absurd act of framing, and I know it. *The Bodyguard* might just be what it looks like: a swoony star vehicle with a killer soundtrack and Kevin Costner being stoic. To call its romance "the cover story over a transaction in mortality" is to do to the movie exactly what Frank does to that house — wire it shut, control every entrance, let nothing in that I didn't plan for. That's the joke we keep. "The Body Bought to Be Killable" wins tonight. It does not win forever — only until a reading walks in that can cross even the scene *it* had to muscle past: the simple, stubborn fact that, against all the theory, the man and the woman really do seem to love each other.
