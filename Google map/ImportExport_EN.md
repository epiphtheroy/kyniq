# Import/Export (2007, Ulrich Seidl) — Tri-Perspective Spatial Abstracts

*Three High-Density Spatial Abstracts on the relationship between the film's diegetic spaces and the reality of its production. (Prompt v2: journalistic voice.)*

---

## Perspective I

### Two Directions, One Wound
**EU Migration Geography in *Import/Export*'s East–West Axis**

Ulrich Seidl's *Import/Export* (2007) is built on a single brutal symmetry: Olga, a nurse from Ukraine, travels west to Austria seeking a better life; Pauli, an unemployed Austrian, travels east installing gaming machines. The film was shot along that exact fault line — in Vienna, Ukraine, Slovakia, the Czech Republic, and Romania, between 2005 and May 2007 — the years bracketing the European Union's great eastward enlargement (Slovakia and the Czech Republic joined in 2004, Romania in 2007), with Ukraine left outside the new frontier. The diegetic migration thus traces the EU's real economic gradient: care work and hope flowing one way, cheap goods and desperation the other. Seidl filmed the actual geography of this divide rather than abstracting it, grounding each humiliation in a real place on the map of European inequality. Reading the locations, *Import/Export* becomes a document of the enlarged EU's internal hierarchy, where a continent's borders are also the borders of dignity. The film's despairing two-way traffic is the human cost of an economic order, made visible on the very terrain that produced it.

## Perspective II

### Real Bodies on the Ward
**Documentary Ethics in *Import/Export*'s Vienna Geriatric Ward**

The most unsettling power of *Import/Export* comes from its refusal to fake. When Olga, the migrant nurse, takes a job cleaning a geriatric ward, Seidl shot those scenes in an actual Vienna hospital — the Lainz geriatric facility — with real, frail elderly patients rather than actors. The unemployment-office sequences likewise used real unemployed people; beyond a couple of professionals, the cast is largely amateurs playing versions of themselves. The diegetic theme — bodies at the bottom of Europe's care economy, the old and the migrant alike treated as surplus — is rendered through the literal presence of those bodies. This documentary-fiction fusion grants the film a devastating authenticity while raising hard ethical questions about consent, dignity, and the camera's claim on dying strangers. Reading the production reframes *Import/Export* as a work made of real institutions and real human vulnerability — not depicted suffering but enlisted suffering. Seidl's method collapses the gap between representation and reality: the nursing home onscreen is the nursing home, and the film's unbearable honesty is also its moral hazard. Location and ethics here are inseparable.

## Perspective III

### A Palace of Garbage
**Roma Poverty in *Import/Export*'s Slovak Housing Estate**

One of *Import/Export*'s bleakest passages sends Pauli and his stepfather into a decayed socialist-era housing block taken over by Roma families — filmed at a real, notorious estate, the Luník IX settlement on the edge of Košice in Slovakia, where collapsed services left no working plumbing and refuse thrown from windows. The diegetic descent into Europe's most marginalized underclass was staged in the genuine site of that marginalization, among its actual residents. Here the film's East–West story bottoms out in a third space: not a nation but an internal exclusion zone, the Roma ghetto that EU enlargement did nothing to lift. Seidl confronts the place directly, his camera implicating the Austrian visitors — and the viewer — in a tourism of misery. Reading this location exposes the limit of the European project: prosperity's expansion eastward bypassed entire populations, leaving segregated estates as durable as borders. *Import/Export* becomes a geography of abandonment, where the continent's poorest are fixed in crumbling concrete while goods and labor circulate around them. The estate is real, and so is its exile.
