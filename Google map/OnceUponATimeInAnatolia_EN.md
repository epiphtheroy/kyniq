# Once Upon a Time in Anatolia (2011, Nuri Bilge Ceylan) — Tri-Perspective Spatial Abstracts

*Three High-Density Spatial Abstracts on the relationship between the film's diegetic spaces and the reality of its production. (Prompt v2: journalistic voice.)*

## Perspective I

### The State Drives Into the Dark
**Bureaucracy and Periphery in *Once Upon a Time in Anatolia*'s Keskin**

*Once Upon a Time in Anatolia* (2011) follows a convoy — a prosecutor, a doctor, police, gendarmes, and a confessed killer — searching the night steppe for a buried corpse near Keskin, a district of Kırıkkale Province in Central Anatolia. The diegesis is, at root, the centralized Turkish state groping through its own rural periphery, and the film was shot precisely there, across eleven weeks in the actual Keskin countryside. The story's authority is not invented: co-writer Ercan Kesal drew on his years as a state-assigned doctor in rural Turkey, where young professionals are posted by the capital to underserved provinces. The bureaucratic ritual onscreen — paperwork demanded over a body in a field, an autopsy in an ill-equipped morgue — reflects the real administrative geography of a country governing vast distances from Ankara. By filming in the genuine hinterland the plot describes, Ceylan fuses fiction to the lived texture of provincial officialdom. The film becomes a map of state power at its frayed edge: institutions, exhausted men, and procedure stretched thin across an immense, indifferent landscape where the center's writ arrives slowly, if at all.

## Perspective II

### An Emptiness Full of People Leaving
**Depopulation in *Once Upon a Time in Anatolia*'s Steppe**

The film's defining image is the Anatolian steppe at night — endless rolling dark, a few distant lights, a lonely village where the convoy pauses. This emptiness is real terrain around Keskin in Kırıkkale, part of the depopulating agrarian interior of Central Anatolia from which generations have migrated toward Ankara, Istanbul, and abroad. The diegetic desolation — men who seem stranded where time forgot them, a village mukhtar pleading for a proper morgue and lamenting the young who keep leaving — corresponds to documented rural flight. Ceylan does not dress the landscape up; he films its actual scale and silence, letting the steppe's vacancy carry the weight of decline. The extradiegetic reality of internal migration is thus inscribed directly into the mise-en-scène: the spaces look empty because the countryside is, in fact, emptying. Reading the location, *Once Upon a Time in Anatolia* becomes more than a procedural; it is a portrait of the Turkish interior as a hollowing periphery, where a single night's search across the dark hills doubles as an elegy for a vanishing rural world.

## Perspective III

### A Border Drawn in Money
**Co-Production Geography in *Once Upon a Time in Anatolia***

A nearly three-hour, slow, dialogue-dense Turkish film about a nocturnal body search is a hard commercial sell, and its financing reflects the realities of art cinema from a semi-peripheral industry. *Once Upon a Time in Anatolia* was produced by Turkey's Zeyno Film in co-production with the Bosnian company Production2006 Sarajevo — a pairing of two countries on Europe's edges, pooling resources to mount a prestige auteur work. The extradiegetic geography of funding thus reaches beyond Turkey's borders even as the story stays rooted in one Anatolian district. That cross-border backing helped carry the film to the 2011 Cannes Film Festival, where it shared the Grand Prix with the Dardenne brothers' *The Kid with a Bike*, consecrating Ceylan within the European festival system that sustains such cinema. Reading the production economy reframes the film: an intensely local, nearly untranslatable night in rural Kırıkkale was made possible by transnational regional capital and validated by a French festival. *Once Upon a Time in Anatolia* is at once deeply Anatolian and a creature of the European co-production circuit that finances the world's slow films.
