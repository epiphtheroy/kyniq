# The Long Way Round
*(DB Protocol v2 — shorter, brisker, fact-dense)*

Ryūsuke Hamaguchi's *Drive My Car* (2021) runs 179 minutes and makes you wait forty of them before the title card appears — a power move, or a dare. By then you have met Yūsuke Kafuku (Hidetoshi Nishijima), a Tokyo stage actor who would always rather be driven than arrive, and his red Saab 900, the second-most expressive performer in the film. You have also met his wife, Oto (Reika Kirishima), a screenwriter who dreams up her plots mid-coitus and forgets them by breakfast, leaving her husband to file them — a marriage run, essentially, as an unpaid stenography service. Then Oto dies of a brain haemorrhage, the cruellest kind of editor, cutting the scene moments before a confrontation Kafuku has spent the whole marriage dodging. Adapted from a Murakami short story barely forty pages long, the film blows that ember into three hours. It is the rare expansion that deepens rather than pads.

What survives Oto is her voice. She recorded herself reading Chekhov's *Uncle Vanya* aloud so her husband could rehearse at the wheel, leaving two-second gaps for his lines — so Kafuku now spends his commute in dialogue with a dead woman who never improvises. It is the eeriest love object in recent cinema and the most practical: grief on a cassette, rewindable. Hamaguchi knows that a recording keeps the soul long after it loses the body; the breath survives, the inflection survives, the wife does not. Charlotte Wells found the same idea a year later in *Aftersun* (2022), where a daughter interrogates camcorder footage of her father. In both films the machine becomes the confidant the living person never managed to be. Kafuku cannot press stop — to do so would admit the marriage is over, and he is not a man who finishes things.

The job that drags him out of his fog is a residency in Hiroshima, directing that same *Uncle Vanya* — a multilingual version performed in Japanese, Korean, Mandarin, and Korean Sign Language at once. His method is borderline sadistic: weeks of table reads in which the actors recite in a dead monotone, forbidden to act, until the words mean nothing and therefore everything. This is, not coincidentally, more or less how Hamaguchi himself rehearses. Chekhov finished *Uncle Vanya* in 1898, about a man grieving a wasted life; leaking through the tedium, the play becomes Kafuku's diagnosis, and you stop being able to tell who is directing whom. Theatre here is a slow machine for postponed confession — feed it your evasions at one end, and months later, in a borrowed tongue, the truth drops out the other.

The festival saddles Kafuku with a driver against his wishes, and Misaki Watari (Tōko Miura) is the film's quiet revelation: twenty-three, unsmiling, a chauffeur so smooth he forgets the road exists. Hamaguchi grasps why the car confesses people the rehearsal room can't. Two figures in the front seats face the same way, intimate but unable to look at each other — the one arrangement under which certain sentences become sayable. The Saab is a threshold that never settles, neither home nor stage, idling around forty kilometres an hour where the etiquette of eye contact is suspended. Abbas Kiarostami built his Palme d'Or winner *Taste of Cherry* (1997) on the identical hunch: a man drives strangers around Tehran, obliquely begging help to die. *Drive My Car* plays like a long, tender reply to it.

For a film this besotted with talk, it nurses a startling suspicion of talk. Its emotional summit belongs to Park Yu-rim as the mute actress Lee Yoon-a, who plays Sonya in Korean Sign Language and delivers Chekhov's closing speech with her hands on Kafuku's back — a wordless benediction that out-acts every spoken line in the preceding three hours. Hamaguchi's wager is plain: meaning lives beneath language, in the body and the pause, and a stage where no actor fully follows the others is not a Babel but a finer kind of listening. People who share no tongue, the film proposes, may attend to one another more closely than people who do. The hands say what the mouth has spent 179 minutes failing to.

So what does this patient oddity resemble? Not, oddly, its own source — it rewrites Murakami with a freedom close to betrayal, and the betrayal is the point. Lee Chang-dong did the same to a Murakami tale in *Burning* (2018), and the two films turn out to be siblings: the best adaptations keep faith with the spirit by junking the letter. *Drive My Car* keeps stranger company still, kin to anyone — a revenge thriller, even a high-school comedy — driven by that same ruthless instinct to gut a plot and keep its pulse. It belongs, too, to the slow cinema of dead time, lingering on silent meals and longer drives, trusting emotion to settle like sediment rather than detonate on cue. Then it points the Saab north.

The road ends in the snow of Hokkaido, at the ruin of Misaki's childhood home, where two people who each failed to save someone finally say so. Here Kafuku stops being driven and arrives; he confesses to Oto's affairs and his own cowardice, and the voice in the back seat is released from its sentence. The dead woman gets, at last, a grave instead of a glovebox. There is a tidy irony in the film's own fate: a movie about a man who cannot reach an ending took three prizes at Cannes in 2021, then four Oscar nominations, winning Best International Feature and becoming the first Japanese film ever nominated for Best Picture — adored, that is, for the grace of an ending its hero never manages. But that was always the Saab's lesson. Some destinations come only the long way round — at idling speed, a ghost riding shotgun — until the drive turns out to have been the arrival.
