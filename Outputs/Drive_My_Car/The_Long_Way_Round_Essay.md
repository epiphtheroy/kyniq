# The Long Way Round

There is a car at the centre of Ryūsuke Hamaguchi's *Drive My Car* (2021), and, this being a film that runs to a luxurious three hours, you have ample time to fall in love with it: a red Saab 900, vintage, lacquered to the colour of a cough sweet, ferrying its owner through tunnels and along coastal roads with the patience of a hearse. Its owner is Yūsuke Kafuku, a Tokyo actor and theatre director, a man who, we slowly realise, would always rather be driven than arrive. The premise is almost perversely quiet. A widower agrees to stage a multilingual production of Chekhov's *Uncle Vanya* in Hiroshima, is assigned a young woman as his chauffeur, and spends a great deal of the picture sitting in the passenger seat saying nothing in particular. And yet the back seat is not empty. A cassette plays — his late wife's voice, reading the other parts of *Vanya* aloud — and that voice, flat and unhurried and dead, is somehow more present in the car than anyone left alive to hear it.

The wife is Oto, a screenwriter who invents her stories during and after sex and forgets them by morning, so that Kafuku must remember them for her, a domestic arrangement that sounds enchanting until you notice that it is also a quiet form of theft. She has affairs; he knows; he says nothing, because to say something might end the marriage he prefers to the truth. Then, one ordinary afternoon, before he can be made to choose, she dies — a brain haemorrhage, the cruellest kind of editor, cutting the scene just before the confrontation. What follows is not grief, exactly. Grief moves; Kafuku has stopped. He has done the very thing the bereaved are warned against: instead of mourning the woman and letting her go, he has swallowed her whole and kept her idling inside him, an engine that never quite turns off. The film is, in one sense, the long account of a man trying to locate the conversation he ducked, and discovering that it has been waiting for him in the one place he cannot stop visiting — the car.

Consider that cassette. Oto recorded it so that Kafuku could rehearse while he drove, feeding him his cues with two-second gaps where his own lines should go, which means he spends his days in conversation with a woman who can no longer answer and never once deviates from the script. It is the eeriest love object in recent cinema, and the most exact: a voice without a body, an acoustic ghost belted into the seat behind him. The two-second gaps are the cruellest detail — spaces shaped exactly like his own lines, a dialogue engineered so that the dead woman will always, forever, leave room for him to answer. What Hamaguchi grasps is that a recording keeps the soul of a person long after it has mislaid the letter — the inflections survive, the breath survives, while the living woman who made them does not. (Charlotte Wells would reach for the same truth a year later in *Aftersun*, where a daughter interrogates camcorder footage of a father she can no longer touch; in both films the machine becomes the confidant the flesh refused to be.) Kafuku cannot switch the tape off. To do so would be to concede that the play, and the marriage, have ended.

The marvel of *Drive My Car* is how it lets Chekhov do the talking the characters cannot. Kafuku's *Uncle Vanya* is no ordinary production; he casts it across languages — Japanese, Mandarin, Korean, and Korean Sign Language — and drills his actors through endless table reads in which they are forbidden to act, made to recite the lines in a deadened monotone until the words stop meaning anything and, paradoxically, begin to mean everything. It is a cruel and beautiful method (and, not incidentally, more or less Hamaguchi's own), a way of wearing the self down until something truer leaks through the tedium. And the play does leak. Vanya's lament — the wasted life, the love that arrives decades too late — stops being a text and starts being a diagnosis, until you can no longer tell whether Kafuku is directing the play or the play is quietly directing Kafuku. Theatre here is a slow machine for delayed confession: feed it your evasions at one end, and months later, in someone else's borrowed language, the truth you have been dodging comes out the other.

But the deepest confessions happen not on the stage but in the Saab, and Hamaguchi understands precisely why. Two people in the front of a moving car face the same direction; they are intimate without being able to look at each other, which turns out to be the only condition under which certain things can be said at all. Misaki, the chauffeur — watchful, unsmiling, a driver so smooth that Kafuku stops noticing the road — becomes his confessor by the simple grace of keeping her eyes forward. The car is a threshold that never settles: neither home nor theatre, neither public nor private, suspended at forty kilometres an hour where the etiquette of the face-to-face is conveniently lifted. (Kiarostami knew this; the whole of *Taste of Cherry* is a man driving strangers around and asking them, obliquely, for permission to die. Hamaguchi's film plays like a long, tender answer to it.) What cannot be spoken across a table can, it seems, be spoken to a windscreen.

For a film so devoted to talk, *Drive My Car* harbours a startling distrust of speech. Its emotional summit belongs to an actress, Lee Yoo-na, who performs Sonya in Korean Sign Language, her hands moving across Kafuku's back in the closing scene of *Vanya* with a tenderness that no spoken line in the picture can match. Hamaguchi is making a wager — that meaning lives beneath language rather than inside it, in the body's signs and silences and in the breath between cues, and that the multilingual stage, where no actor fully understands the others, is not a Babel but a purer form of listening. People who share no tongue, the film proposes, may attend to one another more closely than people who do. It is the same faith that animates the deadpan read-throughs and the wordless drives: strip away fluency, and what remains is the harder, humbler labour of actually being present to another person. The hands say what the mouth has spent three hours failing to.

What, in the end, does this strange and patient film resemble? Not, oddly, the Murakami short story from which it springs, which it expands and rewrites with a freedom bordering on betrayal — and that betrayal is precisely the point. The best adaptations keep faith with a source by breaking faith with its plot, honouring the spirit while cheerfully junking the letter; Lee Chang-dong did exactly this to another Murakami tale in *Burning*, and the two films turn out to be siblings, each translating the writer's cool ambiguity into something more wounded and more human. It keeps stranger company than you might expect, this quiet picture: somewhere on the same family tree sit a Korean revenge thriller and, more improbably still, a Hollywood high-school comedy — relatives not by mood or manners but by the shared, ruthless instinct to gut a source's plot and keep its pulse beating. *Drive My Car* belongs, too, to a rarer family — the cinema of duration, of so-called dead time, where the camera lingers on silent meals and long roads and the unglamorous middle of things, trusting that emotion will accumulate like sediment rather than detonate on cue. It asks of us exactly what it asks of its actors: patience, attention, the willingness to sit with a scene until it surrenders its secret. And then it points the car north.

The road ends in the snow of Hokkaido, at the ruined site of Misaki's childhood home, where two people who have each failed to save someone finally say so out loud. It is here, at last, that Kafuku stops being driven and arrives. He confesses — to Oto's affairs, to his own cowardice, to the fact that he went on performing a contented marriage long after the contentment had curdled — and in doing so he releases the voice in the back seat from its sentence. The tape can be laid to rest because its work is done; the dead woman is given, at last, a grave instead of a glovebox. There is a lovely irony in the film's own fate — much garlanded, thoroughly canonised, an undisputed peak with apparently nothing left to discover: a picture about a man who cannot reach an ending has been embraced precisely for the grace of its own. But that is the lesson the Saab has been teaching from the first frame. Some destinations can only be reached the long way round — driven through, at idling speed, with a ghost for company, until the journey itself turns out to have been the arrival.
