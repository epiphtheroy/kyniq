Why does Nykvist light their faces so harshly?

FilmCurio Editorial · updated 2 days ago · read by 12

The lighting is so intense that sometimes their faces almost blend into the white backgrounds or get lost in deep shadow. What does that extreme contrast do for the story?

The reading

Nykvist's blown-out whites and crushed blacks turn the face into a battleground — and the cruel joke is that the more light he throws at it, the less of the real self we get to see.

His high-contrast photography works like a visual scalpel, stripping away every soft, flattering tone to expose the raw, architectural landscape of a face. In Persona the face is the story's primary battleground, so the lighting fights there: blinding whites on one side, impenetrable black on the other.

Watch what happens at the extremes. When the light is bright enough that the edge of Elisabeth's face dissolves into the background, the image is literally acting out her wish — to vanish, to become nothing. Conversely, the deep shadows swallow the rest of the face, hiding the darker impulses she works so hard to deny. The same frame holds both, which is exactly the split running within and between the two women.

And here's the turn. By over-exposing the film, Nykvist makes their skin a stark canvas that catches every twitch, pore, and micro-expression — total scrutiny. Yet the brighter and harder he lights them, the less of their true features we can actually read. The cinematography stages the film's deepest claim: even under the most brutal looking, the self stays fundamentally obscured.
