# The Sound of the Man

There is a noise at the center of Jafar Panahi's *It Was Just an Accident* that does the work a face usually does in a thriller. A man walks with a prosthetic leg, and the leg squeaks — a small, mechanical, almost apologetic creak, the kind of sound you would take your car to a garage to have silenced. The trouble is that the man who hears it runs the garage, and to him the squeak is not a fault to be greased away but a verdict arriving without a trial. Years earlier, he was held in a prison where the inmates were kept blindfolded, and so they came to know their captors the way bats know a cave: by echo. Faces were confiscated; sounds were not. What the blindfold left behind was a small acoustic dossier, and the squeak is the one entry the body cannot forge. It is also, conveniently, a question the eye can never answer, which is the engine of the entire film — and, one suspects, the secret autobiography of the man who made it, who spent his own stretch of confinement learning to listen to a state he was not permitted to see.

That man has been in trouble with Iran for the better part of two decades, and he has turned the trouble into a method. In 2010 the authorities handed Panahi a sentence of almost theatrical optimism: a twenty-year ban on making films, writing scripts, or leaving the country, as if creativity were a tap one could simply close. He responded by shooting *This Is Not a Film* in his own living room and smuggling it to Cannes on a flash drive baked, the legend goes, inside a cake — surely the only instance of a pastry being submitted to competition. He was jailed again in 2022, at Evin, the address where Iran keeps its embarrassments. *It Was Just an Accident* is the work that followed, and last May it won the Palme d'Or, which Panahi collected in person, having at last been permitted to stand in a country that was not a courtroom. It is, tellingly, his first picture in years in which he himself does not appear. He has stepped out of the frame; the surveillance, this time, points elsewhere.

The premise is the kind of thing a screenwriter would be talked out of for being too neat, except that nothing here is permitted to be neat. Vahid, the mechanic — played by Vahid Mobasseri with the watchful stillness of a man trying not to be recognized himself — becomes convinced that an ordinary customer is Eghbal, the interrogator who once worked on him. The title belongs not to Vahid but to Eghbal, and to every functionary like him; "it was just an accident" is the precise grammar by which states explain the prisoner who fell, who tripped, who died of perfectly natural causes in a building designed to produce unnatural ones. Panahi has named his film after the lie it exists to dismantle, the bureaucratic shrug that launders cruelty into chance. The phrase is so mild, so reasonable, that you could miss the violence folded inside it — which is, of course, the point. Evil here keeps office hours and goes home to dinner.

And then the film does the cruelest thing a revenge story can do to its avenger: it withholds the proof. The face is never confirmed. Vahid's certainty arrives in the gut — a clench, a wave of nausea — and never quite makes the journey up to the head, where evidence is supposed to live. This is the film's true subject, the unbearable gap between conviction and knowledge, and it is the oldest problem in moral philosophy dressed in mechanic's overalls. To punish the man might be justice; to punish the wrong man is to become the thing you are punishing. The squeak is all Vahid has, and a squeak is not a confession. He has built his life around the repair of broken machines, which turns out to be a poor preparation for the one job that matters, since the wound he carries is not the kind that responds to a wrench. He fixes cars because he cannot fix himself, and the customer in his bay has driven in with the one engine trouble he can neither diagnose nor leave alone.

Where do you take a man you intend to make disappear? Panahi's answer is a stretch of scrubland outside Tehran, where Vahid digs a shallow grave and then keeps returning to it, the way a tongue returns to a bad tooth. The hole is finished long before anyone has decided who goes in it, and a prepared grave has a way of nudging the undecided toward a decision; emptiness, it seems, abhors a vacancy. But the desert is doing double duty. Iran has its own unmarked pits — the prison massacres of 1988, the disappeared of every crackdown since — and Vahid's compulsive little excavation cannot help but echo a national one, a country circling the ground it has buried its crimes in, unable either to fill the hole or to walk away. There is no court he can appeal to, because the court is the thing that hurt him. So justice gets improvised in the back of a van, among people whose word counts nowhere official: a tribunal convened without authority, on the reasonable grounds that the authorities will never convene one themselves.

This is where Panahi springs his quietest and most devastating complication, and it is sitting in a parked car the whole time. The suspect's wife is there, heavily pregnant, entirely unaware that her husband's afternoon has acquired a grave. Every time the men steel themselves, she reappears — about to give birth, blameless — and the abstraction called justice collides with a particular life it is about to ruin. A cradle and a grave have been booked for the same afternoon. To complete the vengeance is to orphan a child who has committed no crime, which is to manufacture a fresh victim, which is precisely the trick the regime performs for a living. The unborn child becomes the moral hostage of the plot, the small unanswerable argument that no one in the van can quite talk over. Finish the job, and the avengers become indistinguishable from the men who made them avengers. She never pleads; she simply exists, and her existence is the most eloquent case for mercy in the film.

What, in the end, does a wronged man owe the future? Panahi has been here before, in spirit, and so has a Bosnian cellist named Vedran Smailović, who in 1992 carried his instrument into the rubble of Sarajevo and played Albinoni for twenty-two days, one for each person killed queuing for bread — a gesture so useless and so necessary that it has outlived the siege. The mechanic and the cellist are the same man under different skies: ordinary people insisting on a human gesture exactly where the arithmetic of revenge looks unanswerable. That Panahi can hold this thought without varnishing it is the measure of the film. It would be cozy to add that the world has rewarded him for it, and the world has — the prizes, the reviews, the Criterion seal of immortality. One should only note, with the faint discomfort the film itself invites, that there is a Western appetite for the heroic Iranian dissident, a hunger to applaud his suffering from a safe distance and call the applause solidarity. The Palme d'Or is a genuine honor and, very slightly, an absolution we have written for ourselves.

None of which dulls the thing itself, shot in stolen time by a man the state told to stop, every frame charged with the audacity of merely existing. Panahi refuses us the verdict on purpose. We never see the cell, never witness the crime, never get the confession that would let us go home settled — because settled is the one thing the survivors are not, and the film declines to grant the audience a comfort it denies its characters. We are left, as they are, holding a sound and asked to decide. The squeak does not resolve into a face, or a sentence, or a body in a hole. It just goes on, faint and mechanical, after the lights come up: the noise of a man who may or may not be the man, walking away into a country that has perfected the art of never quite being heard. It was, the title assures us, just an accident. The film spends a hundred and four minutes politely declining to believe a word of it.
